The one-varṇa position

Likewise, the very idea of a single varṇa is a manifestation of ignorance of the purport of the śāstras. The claim is made: “Brahman indeed was this in the beginning, one only; being one, it did not flourish. It created a more excellent form, the Kṣatra … It did not flourish; it created the Viś …” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.11–12). From this statement, it is said, we learn that in the beginning only the Brāhmaṇa existed, and the Kṣatriya and the others did not. Since it was capable of protecting and performing such functions, Brahman created the Kṣatriya, distinguished by strength, prowess, and valour. Hence, from “Brahman is the source of the Kṣatra” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.11), it is understood that the primordial Brāhmaṇa alone was the source of the Kṣatriyas. It then created the Vaiśya for agriculture, trade, and related work, and created the Śūdra varṇa for service. The claim is that in each case the Brāhmaṇa community assigned the Kṣatriyas and the rest according to their aptitude.

That, too, is trivial, because it contradicts the text. What is established here by Brahman being the source of the Kṣatriya womb is only that Brahman produced the Kṣatriyas. It does not establish that certain people among the Brāhmaṇas were designated Kṣatriyas according to their aptitude. There is no authority for such an interpretation, and the verb sṛj means “to create” or “produce.” Earlier, this same writer said that Brāhmaṇas and the others are not varṇas because the word varṇa does not occur alongside their names. Now he says that in the Kṛta age the Brāhmaṇa alone was the varṇa. Yet in the quoted Śruti the designation “Śūdra varṇa” is plainly present.

In truth, priority in the order of creation is natural. That does not make it possible to deny those who come later. Before the creation of wind and the other elements, space alone existed, and from it wind and the rest came into being. This does not prove that wind and the other elements are not distinct elements. In the same way, the Brāhmaṇa existed first, and the Kṣatriyas and the others arose later. That does not make the existence of a single varṇa an established fact, for that very passage states that the varṇas came from Brahman’s mouth and the other parts. Their production is not merely figurative, since that would contradict the statement that Kṣatriyas and the others arose from Brahman just as wind and the rest arose from space. In accordance with the Śruti, “The Rājanya was made from his arms,” the meaning to be understood is that Brahman, in the form of the Brāhmaṇa, had Brāhmaṇas born from his mouth, Kṣatriyas from his arms, Vaiśyas from his thighs, and Śūdras from his feet.

By this same reasoning, the passage “Brahman brought forth the gods; Brahman is this whole universe; from Brahman Kṣatra was fashioned, and Brahman was fashioned from Brahman itself” (Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 2.8.8.6) is also explained. Here, too, Brāhmaṇa-hood belongs to Virāj, the source of Hiraṇyagarbha, and from him the gods are said to have arisen. The interpretation that “the learned are the gods” proves that only learned people are gods is also trivial. If that were so, the statement of Vālmīki, “The learned are Brahmarākṣasas,” would entail that learned people become Brahmarākṣasas. The point here is neither to prescribe that learned people are gods nor that they are Brahmarākṣasas; it is that gods are learned, and Brahmarākṣasas, too, can be learned. “These learned Brahmarākṣasas seek out a flaw” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa 12.18). Thus what is intended is the arising of the gods, the Brāhmaṇas, and the other varṇas from Brahman, Hiraṇyagarbha. The purport of the Mahābhārata passage has already been explained: the world was formerly of one varṇa, and in later births the same beings acquired Kṣatriya-hood and the like through differences in qualities and actions.

The statement, “Formerly there was one Veda, the syllable Oṃ, comprising all speech; the god was Nārāyaṇa, none other, and fire was of one kind” (Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva), describes the state before Kṣatriyas and the others arose; it does not deny their later existence. It is likewise appropriate to say that before the Vedas took their developed form and were transmitted through their branches, Oṃ alone was the Veda. Otherwise, statements such as “Speech is eternal” (Ṛg Veda Saṃhitā 8.75.6) and “This speech was known to those before us” (Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 2.12.6.2) would contradict the claim that the speech expressed in mantras is eternal.

The Vāyu Purāṇa says that in the Kṛta age there was no performance of good or evil actions, no arrangement of varṇa and āśrama, and no mixture. All beings were free from desire and aversion, equal in form and lifespan, with no higher or lower. This describes an age of pure sattva before the varied creation. It cannot be projected onto the present, where desire and aversion, differences in appearance and lifespan, and distinctions of higher and lower are plainly seen.

One may say that, from the standpoint of ultimate reality, because everything is a manifestation of Brahman, its identity with Brahman alone is ultimately real. “All this indeed is Brahman; one should worship it with the thought, ‘From it one is born, by it one is sustained, into it one is dissolved’” (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.14.1). But this does not cancel the practical order of the world or the distinctions of jāti and the like within ordinary experience. Thus, in the Mahābhārata, the fifth Veda, the story of Mataṅga explicitly explains Brāhmaṇa-hood as birth-based.

Even if varṇas were one in the Kṛta age, one cannot deny their existence in the Tretā and later ages, just as one cannot deny the distinct, scripturally established fires used in the Agnihotra. Dharma also differs according to time and place. An Agnihotra offering prescribed for the morning is not a religious duty at midday. In the same way, a duty prescribed in the Kṛta age need not be a duty in the Tretā or Kali age. A duty prescribed for the stage of brahmacarya is not thereby a duty for the householder; a householder’s duty is not thereby a duty for a renunciant. The arrangement described for the Kṛta age was appropriate then, not for the present age.

The Vāyu Purāṇa further says that those who spoke truly of Brahman were Brāhmaṇas; Kṣatriyas were established to protect the others; the Vaiśya’s work was assigned to those among the others who were weak; and those who were sorrowful, fleeing, devoted to service, and lacking in vigour, form, and strength were called Śūdras (Vāyu Purāṇa, Upodghāta-pāda 8). In accordance with the Śruti and Smṛti already cited, the qualities and actions appropriate to each womb are to be understood as the basis on which beings are born into Brāhmaṇa and other wombs. The Śruti says that those whose conduct is pleasing enter a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, or Vaiśya womb.

It is also claimed that in the mantra “The Brāhmaṇa was his mouth,” the words for mouth and the other limbs are figurative, indicating qualities that arise from them. The first-case ending seen there is to be understood in the sense of the fifth case. Knowledge, humility, truthfulness, pleasing speech, and instruction are the principal qualities and actions associated with the mouth; from these, the Brāhmaṇa arose. Strength, energy, and valour belong to the arms; from them arose the Kṣatriya, devoted to protecting those in danger. The thighs are the middle part; from their qualities—earning and distributing wealth and grain, and generosity—and from agriculture, trade, cattle-keeping, and similar work, the Vaiśya arose. From the feet come such inferior qualities as dullness, stupidity, and toil, and such work as service and assistance; from these arose the dependent Śūdra, marked by the inferior qualities and work of service. Service and labour are not themselves inferior qualities, but because inferiority is superimposed on them through the association with dullness and stupidity, they are to be understood as inferior too.

This interpretation is baseless. There is no reason to abandon the primary meaning and impose a figurative one. Manu and others explicitly say that the Brāhmaṇas and the others arose from the mouth and the other limbs. Even if one accepts that Brāhmaṇas and the others arose from qualities and actions associated with the mouth and the other limbs, this does not harm the position that varṇa is rooted in birth: the word “arose” still concerns birth. It is accepted that beings are born into Brāhmaṇa and other wombs because of qualities and actions from previous births. If “arose” is instead made to mean “were figuratively assigned,” the primary meaning has again been abandoned without basis, and the explanation becomes needlessly complicated.

Besides, are knowledge, humility, truthfulness, and pleasing speech qualities and actions found only among Brāhmaṇas? They are desired in all varṇas. How has it been established that dullness and stupidity are qualities of the feet? This has not been explained. The claim that service and labour are inferior qualities also calls for examination. If inferiority is merely superimposed on them, why not superimpose strength and valour on the feet as well? The statement in the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa does not contradict our position. The Ṛg Veda says, “The moon was born from the mind.” Since the moon is the mouth of Virāj, the Brāhmaṇas born from Virāj’s mouth are called “born from Soma.” The Purāṇas say that the Sun is the arm of the Lord; therefore the Kṣatriyas, born from the arms, are called “born from the Sun.” The Purāṇas also describe the ocean as the Lord’s belly; therefore the Vaiśyas born from the Lord’s belly are called “born from the ocean.” Skill in crafts and the like is attributed to the feet because Śūdras are entitled to develop skill in crafts and similar work.

It has also been said, relying on a passage in the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa, that no evidence of difference is found in the forms, intellect, qualities, or defects of Brāhmaṇas and the other groups, while evidence opposing such difference is found in their common origin. On this basis, some advocate abolishing jāti, including Brāhmaṇa-hood, in order to establish a society without classes. The text says: “Brāhmaṇas are not white as moonbeams; Kṣatriyas are not the colour of the kiṃśuka flower; Vaiśyas here are not like yellow orpiment; and Śūdras are not black as charcoal” (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41.41). If Brāhmaṇa-hood depended on being fair and white, only Europeans would be Brāhmaṇas. Some Vedic people in the Madras region, especially young women, have black hair and dark complexions; often only their teeth appear white. A Śūdra may also be fair-complexioned and, by clear pronunciation, may appear just like a Brāhmaṇa. The skin, flesh, fat, and bones do not differ.

Another passage says: “In bodily measure, form, conception, speech, intellect, sense organs, lifespan, strength, the three aims of life, disease, and medicine, there is no distinction made by jāti.” It compares four sons of one father, all of one jāti, and continues: “Since all beings have one father, and are alike through having one father, there is no difference of jāti among them” (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41.43–45). It also says that fruits of one kind of fig tree, whether growing near the trunk, in the middle, or at the top, are alike in colour, shape, touch, and taste, and so one should conclude that there is one jāti (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41.46). It lists a further series of supposed absences of difference: in external and internal features, happiness, wealth, knowledge, fearlessness, strength, form, senses, occupation, lifespan, bodily health, weakness, steadiness, agility, wisdom, dispassion, dharma, valour, skill, appearance, medicine, birth, movement, bodily waste, affection, measure, and hair. “Even when carefully examined in all these respects, the difference between Śūdra and Brāhmaṇa is not seen, even by the assembled gods” (Bhaviṣya Purāṇa 1.41).

That, too, is trivial. In the Mahābhārata, the fifth Veda, Kaśyapa tells Garuḍa that a person whose throat burns upon hearing the Veda is to be recognized and protected as a Brāhmaṇa. Therefore, Brāhmaṇa-hood is to be understood as birth from faultless Brāhmaṇa parents. The Mahābhārata’s account of Karṇa and Paraśurāma says that extraordinary endurance of suffering is a mark of a Kṣatriya. It is also said elsewhere that being born from the powers represented by the Lord’s mouth and the other limbs is what makes one a Brāhmaṇa and the other varṇas.
