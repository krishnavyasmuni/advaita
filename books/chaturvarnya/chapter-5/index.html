Discussions concerning varṇa

The Dīrghatamas account

\n

An objection cites the story of the sage Dīrghatamas, who was blind and old, and whose sons were born from a Śūdra woman. The author says the episode does not establish that action alone determines varṇa. In his reading, the sage’s extraordinary ascetic power enabled Brāhmaṇa sons to be born even from a Śūdra woman; that is an exceptional event, and the text does not present it as a general rule. The objection that the sage could have used this power to restore his own sight and youth is answered by saying that a perfected sage’s own desire governs whether such powers are used. Vyāsa likewise agreed to produce heirs for Dhṛtarāṣṭra only on the condition that the queen could endure his appearance and scent.

Kākṣīvān and Paraśurāma

\n

The story of Kākṣīvān is treated in the same way. The Matsya Purāṇa says that, after long austerity, he attained Brāhmaṇahood by casting off a body associated with a lower birth. The author replies that the account explicitly describes austerity removing the obstructing condition before Brāhmaṇa status becomes manifest; it therefore fits his account of exceptional purification and does not prove that occupation determines varṇa.

Kṣatriya births after Paraśurāma

\n

The author also rejects the claim that Paraśurāma turned fishermen, Niṣādas, and other groups into Brāhmaṇas after destroying the Kṣatriyas. He says it is improper to attribute an act contrary to dharma to the divine incarnation who came to protect dharma. In his interpretation, Paraśurāma purified people who were already Brāhmaṇas, causing their Brāhmaṇa status to become manifest.

Another objection points to the rebirth of Kṣatriya sons after Paraśurāma had made the world “without Kṣatriyas.” The Varāha Purāṇa says that Brāhmaṇas who had mastered the Veda fathered children with Kṣatriya women, and that those children were known as the sons of the women’s husbands. The author says this reflects niyoga, which in certain ages permitted a woman’s husband or family to obtain an heir from a man of the same or a higher lineage. Because the sages acted under a specific scriptural arrangement, the episode does not establish widow remarriage or a work-based varṇa system. He cites Manu’s rejection of remarriage to a widow (Manu 5.65 in the printed citation) and Manu’s condemnation of niyoga as a practice likened to animal behavior (9.66).

The argument then turns to Arjuna’s fear in the Bhagavad Gītā that, when women are corrupted, “mixture of varṇas” arises (Gītā 1.41). The author says Kṛṣṇa answered by urging Arjuna to perform his Kṣatriya duty, not by accepting the premise that a widow’s remarriage or varṇa mixing was dharma. He asks whether the Pāṇḍavas themselves would be called mixed-varṇa, since their births involved divine fathers and the arrangements made by Vyāsa. He answers that those births occurred through scripturally sanctioned, exceptional means: Dhṛtarāṣṭra’s sons were born to Kṣatriya queens, and the Pāṇḍavas were born from Kuntī and Mādrī through divine fathers. Their exceptional origins did not remove their Kṣatriya status. The author therefore denies that these examples support ordinary intermixture or a change of varṇa through occupation.

Action, birth, and the mango-seed analogy

The author next distinguishes scriptural accounts of varṇa-mixture from the births of the Pāṇḍavas and Dhṛtarāṣṭra’s sons. He says those births occurred under prescribed arrangements and within Kṣatriya families, so they do not establish an ordinary intermixture of varṇas. He cites a verse saying that mixture arises through taking up another’s duties, desire, or abandoning one’s own work; he reads it as referring to conduct contrary to dharma, not as proof that a person’s varṇa changes in the present life.

An objection cites the Mahābhārata: “One who, having attained Brāhmaṇahood, undertakes the Kṣatriya dharma, falls from Brāhmaṇahood and is born in a Kṣatriya womb.” The author reads “is born in a Kṣatriya womb” as referring to a later birth, not a change during the same life. He applies the same reading to passages about a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, or Vaiśya who abandons his prescribed duties and takes up Śūdra work. Those passages describe a future birth according to action.

He argues that a stable order of duties is needed for social life. Just as shoes should be kept where shoes belong and garments where garments belong, placing each thing anywhere would be disorder. Differences of duty according to varṇa, he says, make it possible to distinguish one’s own dharma from another’s. He quotes the Gītā: “Therefore, let scripture be your authority in determining what ought to be done and what ought not to be done,” and “Better one’s own dharma, though imperfectly performed, than another’s dharma; another’s dharma is dangerous.”

The author points to Manu’s descriptions of people who steal or commit other acts and are born as animals in a later life: a thief of grain becomes a rat; one who steals meat becomes a vulture; and other thefts lead to other animal births (Manu 12.59–62, as cited). He says these are results across births, not changes of varṇa in the same life. Likewise, the verse “A man who, through greed, steals pearls, coral, and other jewels is born among goldsmiths” (Manu 12.91) refers, he says, to birth in the goldsmith jāti, not merely to taking up the goldsmith’s work. Thus, statements that someone “becomes a Vaiśya” or another varṇa describe the fruit of actions in a later birth.

The author adds that a varṇa system based on occupation alone would have no rule deciding who should begin which work, and would erase the distinction between one’s own duty and another’s. In an emergency, a person may take up a different livelihood without fault; when the emergency ends, he should return to his prescribed livelihood. Vedic study and rites tied to varṇa, however, do not change in this way: a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, or Vaiśya does not thereby become eligible for every other varṇa’s sacrifices. A person’s rule-bound status and the duties appropriate to it, he says, are fixed by birth.

He also rejects a citation from the Vāyu Purāṇa that the four varṇas were arranged according to their work. Past-life actions, he replies, can explain birth in a Brāhmaṇa or another varṇa while leaving birth as the basis of the present order. This is followed by the mango-seed analogy. A mango tree grown from a sweet mango seed may bear sour fruit if the soil and care are poor; that does not make it a different kind of tree. Likewise, a Brāhmaṇa born from a Brāhmaṇī remains a Brāhmaṇa by birth even if poor training, bad surroundings, or lack of learning prevent the fuller qualities of Brāhmaṇahood from appearing. A person of another birth may display Brāhmaṇa-like qualities, but that does not, in the author’s argument, change the person’s jāti.

Occupation, birth, and the regional argument

Continuing the mango analogy, the author says that a mango tree grown from a sweet seed may bear sweet fruit when it receives suitable care, rich soil, and watering with milk; even a mango tree may bear sour fruit under adverse conditions. Neither result changes the tree’s kind. In the same way, a person born to a Brāhmaṇa father and mother, properly initiated, educated, and raised in a good environment displays the principal qualities of a Brāhmaṇa. A child of Brāhmaṇa birth who is untrained and raised in an unsuitable environment may lack those qualities while remaining Brāhmaṇa by birth. The author compares this to a lion lacking courage: its birth-class remains lion, even when it lacks the lion’s characteristic excellence.

He rejects the argument that present-day occupations show that varṇa is determined by work. Some Brāhmaṇas, he says, are ignorant, tell untruths, or earn their living by selling milk or preparing food; many Kṣatriyas farm, herd cattle, or lack courage; some people outside the Kṣatriya birth-class show great bravery; and some Śūdras have learning, good conduct, and wealth worthy of a Brāhmaṇa. Such cases, in his view, show that personal qualities and occupations do not erase birth-based jāti. He says that the traditional order is meant to support social organization and the welfare of the country, and that scripture—not a modern occupational scheme—establishes it.

The author also disputes claims made in a book titled Bhāratavarṣa meṃ Jātibhed by Kṣitimohan Sen, which he says presents many non-Brāhmaṇas as having been made Brāhmaṇas. He calls the account unfounded and says that the related claims about Kṣatriyas and other groups are likewise conjectural. A king cannot simply appoint someone a Brāhmaṇa to perform a sacrifice, he argues, because the śāstras prescribe Brāhmaṇas as the officiants and make eligibility for Vedic study and rites dependent on a person’s established status.

He rejects a story about the Śākadvīpī Brāhmaṇas as well. Citing the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa’s account of Sāmba, Kṛṣṇa’s son, he says the text describes Śākadvīpa as having four varṇas and the Magas as Brāhmaṇas learned in the Veda and its auxiliaries. It says there was no mixture among them and that Sāmba brought them to establish the worship of the Sun. The author takes this as an account of a distinct community, not evidence that a ruler created Brāhmaṇas from people of another jāti.

He calls claims that Madhvācārya or Rāmānujācārya made Brāhmaṇas from non-Brāhmaṇas equally unreliable. He then answers the objection that, if varṇa were natural and birth-based, it would be found in every country. The Bhāgavata, he says, describes India as the land in which the system of varṇa and āśrama is practiced. Many things are confined to particular places and times: the cuckoo does not sing everywhere, nor does Kashmir saffron grow everywhere; similarly, divine incarnations such as Rāma and Kṛṣṇa are described in India, and the varṇa order may be particular to India. If occupation alone determined varṇa, he adds, that occupational system should be visible everywhere too.

The claim that qualities and work alone establish varṇa is also rejected on the ground that modern professions do not follow the traditional divisions. Scientists, teachers, and scholars do not thereby become Brāhmaṇas by birth; soldiers do not form a Kṣatriya jāti; business owners and traders are not treated as Vaiśyas by birth; and manual workers are not all treated as Śūdras. The author says that the formula “there is no varṇa by birth, only by qualities and actions” conflicts with the Veda, smṛti, and Purāṇas.

Purification and the attainment of a higher birth

An objection lists many routes by which a person of a lower varṇa is said to attain a higher one: Brahmā’s sons are Brāhmaṇas by birth; Bharadvāja, Jābāla, and others became Brāhmaṇas through purification and the cultivation of truth and celibacy; Viśvāmitra attained Brāhmaṇahood through austerity; some did so through knowledge; Kākṣīvān and others through action; and certain people through a sage’s boon or curse. The objection also cites ritual purification, conversion from Buddhism, and stories in which Śakas, Yavanas, or Mlecchas entered the four-varṇa order.

The author replies that these accounts do not supply one general rule by which anyone may change varṇa through occupation. Some, he says, were already Brāhmaṇas by birth; in other cases austerity, a boon, a curse, or a prescribed rite was an exceptional means of removing an obstruction or restoring a status that was already present. He treats the accounts of Bharadvāja and Jābāla as cases of birth-based Brāhmaṇas whose qualities were refined, and the stories of Viśvāmitra and Kākṣīvān as exceptional transformations brought about by a perfected sage’s power. He says that those who entered a Buddhist community from a Brāhmaṇa or other varṇa were later purified by prescribed rites; stories of Śakas and others becoming Brāhmaṇas through ordinary action, he says, are false. A Mleccha may follow general religious duties and become a Hindu in the sense of practicing the dharma taught for all people, but this alone does not give him eligibility for Vedic study, which the author ties to birth-based Brāhmaṇahood.

He cites the statement “Austerity, purity, and birth—these are the marks of a Brāhmaṇa,” along with a verse attributed to Aṅgiras: “By birth, bodily formation, learning, conduct, sacred recitation, and dharma as prescribed, Brāhmaṇahood is established. As a picture gradually takes shape through its many parts, so Brāhmaṇahood is formed through the prescribed rites of purification.” The author reads these statements as describing the refinement and manifestation of Brāhmaṇahood through proper formation, learning, conduct, and rites, not as saying that occupation by itself changes jāti. He compares the result to a lion born in a lion’s womb and displaying the full qualities of a lion: austerity and learning bring the principal qualities of Brāhmaṇahood to a person of Brāhmaṇa birth.

“Womb,” jāti, and figurative descriptions

The author rejects the objection that yoni means only a pure mother and a well-formed womb, not a birth-class. He points to expressions such as “a Brāhmaṇa womb” and “a Kṣatriya womb,” and says that in such usages yoni clearly means jāti. He cites Manu’s phrase “birth from the mother’s womb” (Manu 2.169) in support.

He allows that texts may call someone a Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, or Śūdra because of that person’s qualities or work, but says these are secondary, figurative usages. A person may be called a “lion” because of courage without being born a lion; similarly, qualities can support a secondary description without replacing birth-based status. He cites verses in the Atri Smṛti that call a person a sage because of forest-dwelling and devotion, or a Brāhmaṇa because of Vedānta study and detachment; they call a warrior a Kṣatriya, a farmer or cattle-keeper a Vaiśya, a person engaged in certain trades a Śūdra, and people who fish, hunt, deceive, or act cruelly by other names. The author says these are names based on qualities and conduct. If varṇa were determined only by work, he asks, how could people of different birth-groups display the qualities named in those verses? He concludes that birth establishes the primary varṇa, while qualities can produce secondary descriptions.

The stages of a Brāhmaṇa boy’s education are also given names in the Baudhāyana Gṛhya Sūtra: before initiation he is called jāta; after investiture with the sacred thread and the beginning of vows, brahmacārin; after learning some of the Veda, Brāhmaṇa; after studying one branch, śrotriya; after studying the auxiliary texts, anūcāna; after studying the ritual manuals, kalpī; and after studying the four Vedas, a sage. The author stresses that these names apply to a boy born of Brāhmaṇa parents; they are not names that any child may acquire merely by study or conduct. Purificatory rites and good qualities refine the status but do not, in his account, establish varṇa independently of birth.

Social custom, dharma, and the Gītā

The author notes that a Śūdra with good conduct may be better than a Brāhmaṇa who has abandoned proper conduct, and quotes a Purāṇic verse to that effect. He says this praises good behavior; it does not deny birth-based jāti. In ordinary social practice, he argues, birth continues to govern marriage, inheritance, and other relationships. Even reformers who disregard birth in marriage still rely on it in inheritance disputes, and a person with excellent qualities is not thereby treated as a biological son eligible to inherit in place of an actual son.

He rejects the further claim that modern education and occupations have made varṇa distinctions impossible to observe. Students from different communities may study the same subjects, dress alike, eat together, adopt Western customs, and form friendships or marriages across communities; yet, he says, birth-based distinctions remain in family relations and inheritance. Nor does he accept the claim that widow remarriage and varṇa-mixture have become the “dharma of the age.” Dharma is defined by Vedic injunction, he says, not by present custom. Those who do not trust the Lord’s ordering of the ages may dismiss birth-based varṇa, but religious people should work to restore the dharma taught by śruti and smṛti even if success is not immediately visible. He quotes the Mahābhārata: “If a person strives to do what is right with his full strength but does not attain the result, he nonetheless obtains the merit; of this there is no doubt.”

He cites verses urging people to restore the Vedic path: “Establish me with effort, O Brāhmaṇas; when the Vedic path is obscured, the whole world becomes unstable. One who is able to restore it but fails through delusion is no less blameworthy than its destroyer. One who is intent on restoring it, even if unable, becomes free of sin and attains true knowledge.” He then says that education and work do not themselves cause varṇa: Kṣatriyas who study or teach do not become Brāhmaṇas, and Brāhmaṇas such as Droṇa do not become Kṣatriyas merely because they are skilled in warfare.

The author next rejects the claim that Kṛṣṇa remained silent about Arjuna’s fear of varṇa-mixture because it would be beneficial rather than harmful. He says that interpretation has no basis and conflicts with the Gītā’s insistence that one’s own dharma is the authority. If Arjuna were to abandon his Kṣatriya duty and live by begging because he feared the loss of relatives, others would follow his example and abandon their own duties. If he instead stood firm in his duty, widows would remain faithful to their husbands or undertake celibacy and austerity, rather than become immoral. The author points to the women of Mewār, whose husbands, brothers, fathers, or fathers-in-law died in battle: he says they entered the funeral fire or remained devoted to chastity, and that their conduct did not produce adultery or varṇa-mixture. Where the men were steadfast in dharma, he argues, women followed that example; where the men were corrupt, even wives and unmarried girls became corrupt. He therefore rejects widow remarriage and varṇa-mixture as a new age’s dharma.

Finally, he develops the analogy of the cosmic Person. The head and mouth, arms, thighs, and feet are distinct parts, each with its own function; the capacities associated with knowledge, protection, production, and service likewise have distinct roles. In the human being, all such capacities are present and should be cultivated together. Knowledge and concentration strengthen the head; exercise and food strengthen the chest and arms; the abdomen receives and distributes nourishment; the feet carry a person to the desired place and support the body. The author argues that society needs these different capacities working together. A complete human life requires learning, strength, wealth, and labor; the four aims of life depend on their combination. He sees Kṛṣṇa as displaying all four kinds of qualities: as teacher of the Gītā, as a powerful protector, as a cowherd and steward of wealth and fields, and as a servant who performed practical tasks for the Pāṇḍavas. He concludes that the fourfold order in the cosmic Person is reasonable, and that the capacities of all the limbs should be respected and developed.
