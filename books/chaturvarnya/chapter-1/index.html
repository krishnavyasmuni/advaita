Birth-based varṇa

By the lexicon statement, “varṇa [is used] for a twice-born person and the like, and for white and the like,” the word varṇa is established in the sense of a jāti beginning with the twice-born. The word jāti, however, is indeed rooted in birth. For example, jātis such as lionhood are indeed rooted in birth.

The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth; the Rājanya was made His arms. His thighs were that which was the Vaiśya; from His feet the Śūdra was born.

By this mantra, the arrangement of the varṇas by birth is explicitly explained. Thus: the Brāhmaṇa was the mouth of this Lord whose form is the Great Cosmic Puruṣa; the meaning is, [he was] born from the mouth. The Rājanya was His arms, made [so] because of being born from the arms. The two thighs of this Puruṣa were the form of the Vaiśya. From the feet of this Lord the Śūdra was born.

Now, it may be objected here: because of the expression of co-reference in “the Brāhmaṇa [was] the mouth,” the Brāhmaṇa’s being the mouth of Him may be merely figurative. The same relation should likewise be understood for the Rājanya and the others. This is not so. From the concluding statement, “From His feet the Śūdra was born,” just as the Śūdra’s birth from the feet [is understood], the Brāhmaṇa and the others too must be understood as born from the mouth. Nor should it be said that co-reference is thereby inexplicable: it is established as in “clay-pot” and “gold-earring,” where there is co-reference by being produced from that. Therefore, the great sage Śaunaka, by saying “because it was produced from that,” stated that the Brāhmaṇas and the others take the form of the mouth and the rest precisely through being produced. The Taittirīya Śruti too, by its statement of creation, such as “He fashioned the Trivṛt from the mouth,” has stated the birth-origin of Brāhmaṇa-ness and the rest.

For the increase of the worlds, from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet, he brought forth the Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra. Here Kullūka Bhaṭṭa [says]: “For the abundance of the worlds beginning with Bhū, he created the Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, and Śūdra from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet, in that order. The Brāhmaṇa and the rest were created from the mouth and the rest.” Since the jāti status of Brāhmaṇa-ness and the rest is established for those alone who are born from the mouth and the rest, the well-known conclusion here too fits: jāti is by birth alone. For in the world it is well known that the jāti of lions and the rest is by birth alone. In that way, just as lions and the rest, by virtue of being jāti, are not rooted in qualities, actions, and the like, so too are the Brāhmaṇas and the rest.

The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of guṇa and karma. Know Me, though its maker, as the immutable non-agent.

Now, [it may be objected]: “By the Gītā’s statement, ‘The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of guṇa and karma,’ it is understood that the fourfold varṇa was created by guṇas and karmas alone.” No, because this arises from ignorance of the intended meaning. Thus: just as sticks and the like are instrumental in the production of a pot, guṇas and karmas and the like are said to be instrumental in the creation of the fourfold varṇa. Just as, when a pot has arisen, its continued state is not by sticks and the like, but the sticks and the like that existed before the pot’s production are causes of the pot; in the same way, when bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the rest are attained by birth in the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the rest, guṇas and karmas are causes [of that attainment]. Therefore, only those guṇas and karmas by which the Lord made the bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the rest are stated here to be instrumental causes of the fourfold varṇa. And this is indeed accepted by followers of Sanātana Dharma, since every effect everywhere has guṇa and karma as its root. Therefore the Lord said, “The fourfold varṇa [was created] by Me,” as [were] the sky, Moon, Sun, and the rest. Just as the sky and the rest cannot be created by all, so too the Brāhmaṇas and the rest. But proponents of a fourfold-varṇa arrangement rooted in guṇa and karma desire the determination of Brāhmaṇa-ness and the rest by the guṇas and karmas belonging to those respective bodies after the arising of those respective bodies; that is not established by this statement.

Viśvāmitra account

Nor should it be said that Viśvāmitra, Vasiṣṭha, and others, whose distinction by another jāti is well known from scripture, underwent a change of jāti through the power of their own austerities, and that this must therefore be based on qualities, actions, and the like.

Rather, they were originally Brāhmaṇas and the like. Through the power of their austerities, the obstructing coverings were removed, and their Brāhmaṇa status and the like became manifest.

According to Vālmīki’s account of Viśvāmitra, the exchange of the Brāhmaṇa oblation prepared by the great sage Ṛcīka made it the material cause of Viśvāmitra’s body. Though he was a Brāhmaṇa, austerity and the like were used only to remove the defect of birth in a Kṣatriya womb.

As for Vasiṣṭha, he was indeed a Brāhmaṇa because he was a son of Brahmā. After Nimi’s curse, he was born from the seed of Mitra and Varuṇa, stirred on account of Urvaśī and deposited in a pot. This was merely divine birth and did not remove his Brāhmaṇa status. The matter concerning Devāpi will be stated later.

Śaṅkara’s view

The revered Commentator too stated the origins of the Brāhmaṇas and the others according to the gradations of the differences in the qualities beginning with sattva, intensified by actions.

The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of qualities and actions. Know Me, though its creator, as the immutable non-agent.

“The fourfold varṇa” means the four varṇas. “Created by Me” means produced by Me, the Lord, in accordance with the śruti, “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth,” and so on. “According to the division of qualities and actions” means according to the division of qualities and the division of actions. The qualities are sattva, rajas, and tamas. Among these, the actions of one in whom sattva predominates are calmness, self-restraint, austerity, and the like. The actions of a Kṣatriya in whom rajas predominates, with sattva subordinate, are valor, vigor, and the like. The actions of a Vaiśya in whom rajas predominates, with tamas subordinate, are agriculture and the like. Service alone is the action of a Śūdra in whom tamas predominates, with rajas subordinate. Thus, the meaning is: “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of qualities and actions.” And this fourfold varṇa does not exist in other worlds; hence the specification “in the human world.” This is the commentary of Śaṅkara there.

Here, the qualities beginning with calmness and self-restraint mentioned in the Gītā are stated to be natural to the Brāhmaṇas and the others; but that Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like are based on them is not within the purport of the Lord’s words. Therefore, just as a lion born from a lioness, endowed with qualities such as valor and fierceness, is a primary lion, so among the Brāhmaṇas and the others, those born from Brāhmaṇas and the others and endowed with qualities appropriate to each varṇa, such as calmness and self-restraint, are the primary Brāhmaṇas and the others. Brāhmaṇas and the others who lack the natural qualities and are dependent on birth alone are Brāhmaṇas by birth alone; but those who lack such birth and are endowed with qualities beginning with calmness and self-restraint are Brāhmaṇas and the others only in a secondary sense, by virtue of qualities. Just as a lion lacking qualities such as valor is a lion by kind, while Devadatta and the like, lacking lion birth but endowed with valor and the like, are lions in a secondary sense, so too it is to be understood here.

As the Mahābhāṣyakāra said: “Austerity, sacred learning, and birth make a Brāhmaṇa. He who is deficient in austerity and sacred learning is a Brāhmaṇa by birth alone.”

Purāṇic agreement

Here some say that no accounts are heard in the Purāṇas of any Brāhmaṇas being born from the mouth of the Supreme Lord. Rather, in the Bhāgavata and other Purāṇas, by statements such as “Nārada was born from a limb,” the birth of those particular Brāhmaṇas from Prajāpati’s thumb and other limbs is heard. That is trivial, because in the Mahābhārata and other texts the birth of hundreds of pairs of Brāhmaṇas and the others from the mouth and the other limbs is described. Even if this is so of the original Brāhmaṇas, in other cases what is intended is precisely their origin from the powers of the mouth and the other limbs.

“The Lord created one hundred foremost Brāhmaṇas from His mouth alone; from His arms one hundred Kṣatriyas, and from His thighs one hundred Vaiśyas. From His feet too Keśava created one hundred Śūdras, O best of the Bharatas.”

Here, in statements such as “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth,” the words “mouth” and the like refer only to the respective primary powers. Thus Brāhmaṇas arise from the power of the mouth, and Kṣatriyas from the power of the arms. Although these beings and trees and the like originate by the power of resolve and are not directly womb-born, since birth from the power of a womb is nowhere absent, the womb-born status of all is established. By the word “womb” is understood the power of Prakṛti, which serves as the material cause.

Therefore, the whole universe is of the nature of Śiva and Śakti. Even where there is no union of Śiva and Śakti in the form of a womb and liṅga, their presence as the all-pervading nature of Śiva and Śakti is undeniable even in a sprout and the like.

Jāti through boons and curses

And where, through the boon or curse of great sages, a lion attains divinity or the state of a boar, there, by the process stated above, through the operation of Prakṛti according to differences among classes of powers, the material causes constituting the former body are dissolved, and the material causes that initiate another jāti are assembled. In that case, a change of jāti in that very birth is indeed an exception.

Jāti through cessation of admixture

In some Smṛtis, a change of jāti is also taught in another way. For example, a Śūdra girl, when married by a Brāhmaṇa, may bear a daughter; she too, when married by a Brāhmaṇa, may bear a daughter. In this way, in the seventh generation that girl is regarded as a Brāhmaṇī. In these distinctions, all the statements of the Śāstra are reconciled.

Figurative Śūdratva

What someone has claimed—that “by birth one becomes a Śūdra”—and declared, that everyone is a Śūdra by birth, is also trivial, because no authoritative statement of that kind is found. Rather, opposite statements of Smṛti are found, such as “He is to be known as a Brāhmaṇa by birth” and “A Brāhmaṇa by birth is superior.”

In some places, the intended meaning is only “like a Śūdra.” “One devoid of literature, music, and the arts is a beast indeed, without tail and horns.” Here, a person devoid of literature, music, and the arts does not actually become an animal; the intended meaning is only “like an animal.”

“The Brāhmaṇa who, in particular, does not worship the Sandhyā becomes a Śūdra while still living; when dead, he is born as a dog.” (Dakṣa Smṛti 2.32)

“One who eats the food of a Śūdra continuously for one month becomes a Śūdra in this birth; when dead, he is born as a dog.” (Aṅgiras Smṛti 6.7)

“A twice-born man who, without studying the Veda, applies himself to other pursuits quickly attains Śūdra-hood while still living, together with his descendants.” (Manu Smṛti 2.168)

Similarly, in statements such as “by conduct one becomes a Śūdra,” Śūdra-hood too is to be understood only in a secondary sense. Likewise, in statements such as “the sons of Kṣatriyas became Brāhmaṇas” and “two Vaiśyas attained Brāhmaṇahood,” only likeness to a Brāhmaṇa is to be understood, as with a lion used in a secondary sense. These statements do not bring about the attainment of Brāhmaṇa-hood, Śūdra-hood, and the like, since jāti is eternal and its alteration is impossible as long as the body lasts.

The śruti “On the eleventh day the father should give the name” prescribes on that very eleventh day the giving to Brāhmaṇas and the others of names appropriate to their respective varṇas. At that time, however, Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like cannot be determined in accordance with qualities and actions. Therefore, the Śāstras prescribe the respective rites while designating Brāhmaṇas and the others as those respective varṇas: “One should initiate a Brāhmaṇa at eight years and teach him, a Rājanya at eleven years, and a Vaiśya at twelve years. A Brāhmaṇa should establish the fires in spring, a Rājanya in summer, and a Vaiśya in autumn. A king who desires sovereign rule should sacrifice with the Rājasūya.”

In these and similar statements, the rites are prescribed with reference to varṇas established by birth. They are not stated in this way: “One who wishes to become a Brāhmaṇa should do this; one who does this is a Brāhmaṇa.” No prescription of Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like through the respective actions is heard in that manner.

Just as daughters and the like are established by birth, and the actions appropriate to daughters and the like are prescribed in the world for those established by birth, it is not said, “She who acts in this way becomes a daughter; she who acts in this way becomes a wife.” If that were so, others too could become wives by performing the actions of a wife. The same is to be understood here.

Brāhmaṇa-hood applied to knowledge of Brahman and the like, however, is entirely different from that, because it depends upon jāti and is not itself of the nature of a jāti.

Distinct birth-wombs of Brāhmaṇas and others

Now, [someone may say]: “Just as lions, dogs, and the like differ in jāti according to differences of womb, this is not so with the wombs of Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, and the others.” That is not so, because the Chāndogya teaches differences in the birth-wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others: “Those here whose conduct is pleasing attain a pleasing womb—either the womb of a Brāhmaṇa, the womb of a Kṣatriya, or the womb of a Vaiśya. Those here whose conduct is foul attain a foul womb; they enter the womb of a dog, the womb of a pig, or the womb of an outcaste.” (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 5.10)

Here the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others are described just as the wombs of dogs, pigs, and the like. Therefore, just as there is a difference of jāti between dogs and pigs, there is likewise a difference of jāti among Brāhmaṇas and the others. How could there be a difference of jāti in the absence of distinction? A difference of jāti among Brāhmaṇas and the others, as different kinds of human beings, is established just as among different kinds of horses and the like. “Having become a horse, he carried the gods; having become a stallion, he carried the Gandharvas.”

Moreover, distinction is not seen only in visible form; if it were so, differences in scent and the like could not be accounted for. Although mango trees do not differ in appearance, differences in their fruits and the like are perceived. Even when the differences among many mango fruits are not perceived by appearance, their differences are understood through taste and the like. In the same way, although they do not differ with respect to being human and the like, the distinction of Brāhmaṇas and the others is established through differences in happiness, capacity to delight, and the like.

Therefore, even where Brāhmaṇa-hood is taught with reference to a father and the like, Brāhmaṇa-hood in the father, son, and the others is understood as directly stated. By this, the formation of feminine words such as “Brāhmaṇī” by the feminine suffix after a jāti word is established. In the grammatical rule concerning “Brāhmaṇa” in a non-jāti sense, that form is prescribed when it does not denote a jāti; when it does denote a jāti, “Brāhmaṇa” itself is used.

Therefore: “Even a twice-born of bad conduct is worthy of honor, but not a Śūdra whose senses are controlled. Who, abandoning a wicked cow, would milk a well-behaved donkey?” (Parāśara 8.32)

“Whether unlearned or learned, the Brāhmaṇa is a great divinity; whether ritually prepared or not, fire is a great divinity.” So says Manu.

With reference to the excellence of the birth-womb, if there is a distinction of varṇa by birth, then the difference in naming is consistent: “A Brāhmaṇa’s name should be auspicious; a Kṣatriya’s should be endowed with strength; a Vaiśya’s should be associated with wealth; and a Śūdra’s should be repulsive.”

Guṇa-and-karma doctrine

It is said: “All these words apply to a collection of qualities: Brāhmaṇa, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, Śūdra” (Pāṇini 2.2.6). According to Patañjali’s explanation, the words beginning with Brāhmaṇa are expressions of qualities alone, not words denoting jāti. Accordingly, the arrangement of varṇas rests on a collection of qualities and actions such as austerity and sacred learning. Therefore, the mantra “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth” should properly be explained in accordance with this. The mouth of Brahmā, whose form is the universe, was the person who wished to become a Brāhmaṇa, distinguished by the qualities that cause Brāhmaṇa-hood, abundant in sattva, endowed with learning of the Veda and the like, and devoted to the Supreme Lord. He was foremost and supreme, occupying the place of the mouth. Just as the mouth, being foremost in knowledge, is superior to the other limbs, so too he is called the mouth because he is foremost in knowledge and superior through qualities such as calmness and the rest.

“Now, whoever, knowing this imperishable, departs from this world becomes a Brāhmaṇa.” In śruti passages such as this, the designation “Brāhmaṇa” depends on knowledge.

The Rājanya, distinguished by valor and the like, abundant in rajas with sattva subordinate, was produced by the arms. He was made to occupy the place of the arms because, like the arms, he is chiefly characterized by strength. Likewise, the Vaiśya, occupying the place of the thighs of this Puruṣa, takes their form; he is distinguished by actions such as farming and trade and is chiefly concerned with accumulating wealth. He was made the thighs, abundant in rajas with tamas subordinate. By derivation: “He who repeatedly enters here and there for the sake of work is a Viṭ; his offspring is a Vaiśya.”

The Śūdra, stationed in the place of the feet, is characterized by service, crafts, skill, and labor; he is born with tamas predominant and rajas subordinate. Thus it is imagined that the Brāhmaṇas and the others, endowed with those qualities, exist as the limbs beginning with the mouth of Prajāpati, whose form is the cosmic body. That is not so. The statement “The Brāhmaṇa was His mouth” also teaches Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like as arising from the mouth and the other limbs, as is confirmed by the concluding words, “The Śūdra was born from His feet.” The ablative case cannot be changed into a nominative meaning merely in accordance with the context, since that would oppose the primary meaning and require a figurative meaning.

Is the mouthhood of Brāhmaṇas and the others primary or secondary? If it is a relation of co-reference, is it primary or secondary? Is it figurative, as in “The lion is Devadatta”? Or is it by denial, as in “This pillar is a man”? Is the co-reference primary, as in “That is Devadatta,” or is it only a relation of effect and cause, as in “The pot is clay”? Since the ablative alone, by removing doubt, expresses the intended meaning, it is superior. Just as a pot is called clay because it is made of clay, so too the designation “mouth” is explained through predominance. Therefore the creation account that follows—“The Moon was born from the mind, the Sun was born from the eye”—is consistent. Manu too clearly states that Brāhmaṇas and the others are born from those limbs in “those born from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet.”

Moreover, if calmness and the like cause Brāhmaṇa-hood, then one already endowed with them would already have become a Brāhmaṇa. How, then, could the wish to become one arise in him? If by “Brahman” only the supreme reality that supports all is intended, that too does not fit, because Brahman cannot be born as Brahman, and because it contradicts the śruti that one, being Brahman itself, attains Brahman. If it is said that one who does not know Brahman becomes Brahman through knowledge, that too does not fit, since this is accomplished by discernment and does not depend on Brāhmaṇa-hood. Hence the śruti says: “Among humans, gods, and seers, whoever knows that supreme Brahman becomes Brahman itself. Whoever among the gods awoke to it became that; so too among the seers and humans.”

Moreover, in the cosmic form of Brahmā, the gods alone occupy the position of the mouth, because they predominate through the abundance of sattva and are superior even to humans; humans do not, since they are mortal.

Two meanings of the word Brāhmaṇa

It is said: “Whoever, O Gārgī, departs from this world without knowing this imperishable is a wretched person; but whoever departs from this world knowing this imperishable is a Brāhmaṇa.” In śruti passages such as this, the designation “Brāhmaṇa” is based on knowledge. This does not negate Brāhmaṇa-hood as a jāti, because this śruti concerns the technical meaning of “Brāhmaṇa,” just as the grammatical terms “guṇa” and “vṛddhi” have technical meanings.

Therefore, śruti enjoins effort to attain Brāhmaṇa-hood in the form of steadfastness in Brahman even for one who is a Brāhmaṇa by jāti. “Having known this very Self, the Brāhmaṇas, after rising above desire for sons and desire for wealth, wander as mendicants.” “A Brāhmaṇa, having become indifferent to learning, should seek to remain as a child. Having become indifferent to childlikeness and learning, he should become a sage. Having become indifferent to silence and non-silence, then he is a Brāhmaṇa.”

Here, the first occurrence of “Brāhmaṇa” denotes jāti, while the second denotes one who knows Brahman, because a Brāhmaṇa alone is eligible for renunciation and the like. Here the knower of Brahman is not enjoined to pursue learning, childlikeness, and the like, since these have already been accomplished.

Therefore, the Vajrasūcī Upaniṣad’s teaching of Brāhmaṇa-hood characterized by knowledge of Brahman for someone other than a Brāhmaṇa by jāti does not deny jāti. It says there: “Many seers, such as Agastya, are described as having attained knowledge of Brahman without belonging to this jāti. Therefore, there is no Brāhmaṇa by jāti.” Since that is a cause of liberation, that alone is primary Brāhmaṇa-hood.

By this it is also explained that the Rājanya is predominant in strength because he is born from the arms, which are predominant in strength. Likewise, the Vaiśya and Śūdra are characterized by birth from the thighs and feet. Therefore, Smṛti texts clearly state their birth from the mouth and the other limbs.

“This is the duty of those born from the mouth: bearing the mark of Viṣṇu. This duty is not prescribed for those born from the arms and thighs.”

Manu and others state birth from those limbs in such passages as “those born from the mouth, arms, thighs, and feet.” Their commentators, including Kullūka Bhaṭṭa, likewise state birth from those parts. In every respect, Smṛti follows the meaning of Śruti, as the great poet Kālidāsa said. Therefore, the meaning stated in Smṛti is to be understood as the meaning of Śruti. Any mistaken views contrary to Manu should be rejected.

“Whatever Smṛtis stand outside the Veda, and whatever mistaken views there are, all should be known as fruitless, for those Smṛtis are said to abide in darkness.”

With regard to the statements “The Śūdra was born from His feet” and “[the Rājanya] was fashioned from the arms,” it is not proper to abandon their primary meanings and impose a figurative meaning, such as taking “was born” and “was fashioned” to mean “was imagined.” When the primary meaning is possible, adopting another meaning is unjustified.

Devāpi account

It is also said: “The seer, Ārṣṭiṣeṇa’s son, Devāpi, knowing the gods’ good will, sat as Hotṛ. He sent the heavenly waters from the upper ocean down to the lower, toward the fields. When Devāpi, appointed as priest for Śantanu and chosen as Hotṛ, prayed compassionately for rain, Bṛhaspati gave him a voice heard by the gods, a voice that brings rain.” (Ṛgveda 10.98.5–7)

From these mantras it is understood that Devāpi Kauravya, Śantanu’s brother, through austerity attained Brāhmaṇa-hood, with his Vedic knowledge made manifest, and became a priest. Yāska too interpreted the mantra in the same way. An account is told there: Devāpi Ārṣṭiṣeṇa and Śantanu Kauravya were brothers. Śantanu, the younger, had himself consecrated as king. Devāpi undertook austerity and the Brāhmaṇa’s way of life. Thereafter, for twelve years during Śantanu’s reign, rain did not fall. The Brāhmaṇas who knew the Veda told him: “You have acted unrighteously. You were consecrated after passing over your elder brother; therefore, the gods do not rain for you.” Śantanu then asked Devāpi to accept the kingdom. Devāpi said to him: “I shall be your priest and officiate for you.” This is his hymn of desire; this is its meaning. (Nirukta 2.10)

The summary meaning of the mantra is this: Devāpi, appointed as Śantanu’s priest and chosen as Hotṛ for the rite intended to bring rain, prayed compassionately for the king. Taking him to be one whose speech was heard by the gods and who prayed for rain, Bṛhaspati gave him speech that brings rain. By praising the gods with that speech, he obtained rain from them. This is also supported by the words: “Sit as Hotṛ, worship the gods at the proper time, and serve the gods with oblation, O Devāpi.” (Ṛgveda 10.98.4)

Thus it is said: “The Kauravya Ārṣṭiṣeṇa, firm in his vows, attained Brāhmaṇa-hood through great austerity, O Rāma, best of sages. The royal sage Sindhudvīpa and the great ascetic Devāpi attained Brāhmaṇa-hood; so too did the great sage Viśvāmitra, blessed, of fierce splendour and great austerity.” (Mahābhārata, Rājadharma section)

The account of Viśvāmitra likewise supports the arrangement of varṇas by qualities and actions. It is said: “Born of Gādhi, of great splendour, he blazed like a fire. By austerity he cast off Kṣatriyahood and attained the radiance of Brahman.” (Mahābhārata 6.16.28)

“Then the great ascetic Viśvāmitra attained Brāhmaṇa-hood. Though he too was a Kṣatriya, he became the founder of a line of Brāhmaṇas.” (Mahābhārata, Śānti section 4.8)

Likewise, the eighty-one sons of Ṛṣabhadeva attained Brāhmaṇa-hood through qualities and actions. It is said: “The younger sons, the eighty-one descendants, obeyed their father’s command. Great in bodily strength, great in Vedic learning, devoted to sacrifice and purified in their actions, they became Brāhmaṇas.” (Bhāgavata 5.4.12) “The eighty-one twice-born were promulgators of the ritual tradition.” (Bhāgavata 11.2.19)

Although these too were Kṣatriyas, they became Brāhmaṇas after abandoning Kṣatriya duties through austerity and the like. This does not contradict the birth-based arrangement of Brāhmaṇas and the other varṇas, since a change of jāti takes place between births, not within the same birth; this is the general rule.

Someone may say: “The mantra says that Devāpi, though a Kṣatriya, was Śantanu’s priest; and because he performed the work of a priest, though he was a Kṣatriya, he accepted Brāhmaṇa-hood in that very birth through Brāhmaṇa duties, austerity, and the like. Thus Brāhmaṇa-hood based on qualities and actions is established.” That is a trifling argument, because the initiation and eligibility for the rite are forbidden to one who is not a Brāhmaṇa.

Yāska explains the account in this way: Devāpi and Śantanu, sons of Ṛṣṭiṣeṇa, were Kauravya brothers. Śantanu, the younger, had himself consecrated as king. Devāpi undertook austerity. Thereafter rain did not fall for twelve years during Śantanu’s reign. The Brāhmaṇas who knew the Veda told him: “You acted unrighteously, passing over your elder brother and having yourself consecrated. Therefore, the gods do not rain for you.” Śantanu then entreated Devāpi to take the kingdom. Devāpi said: “I shall be your priest and officiate for you.” (Nirukta 2.10)

Ārṣṭiṣeṇa, son of Ṛṣṭiṣeṇa, was chosen as Hotṛ for the rite intended to bring rain for Śantanu and sat down to perform the Hotṛ function. Devāpi knew the favourable intention of the gods, turned toward granting water. When the gods had been propitiated, he sent the heavenly waters down from the upper ocean to the earthly ocean; this is the distinction between the two oceans. The waters sent forth nourished the crops. As priest and Hotṛ for the rite for rain, compassionating the king, he prayed that rain might fall.

If it is said that the mantra, Nirukta, and its commentary state that Devāpi was a Kṣatriya and also say that he served as a priest, the answer is no. The words “Ārṣṭiṣeṇa’s son” and the rest praise the deity and do not establish that a Kṣatriya attained Brāhmaṇa-hood through priestly work in the same birth. Therefore, Durgācārya, the commentator on the Nirukta, explicitly explains that the Devāpi appointed as Hotṛ in the rite for rain for Śantanu belonged to another kalpa.

Brāhmaṇa-hood through austerity in another birth

It was stated there, according to the principle “the relation between a word and its meaning is innate,” that the relations between words and meanings are natural and eternal. Since humans and others are impermanent, being subject to birth and death, the varṇas are understood as eternal classes, not as individual persons. Vedic words do not denote individuals; they denote eternal jātis. For example, the word “Indra” denotes the jāti of being Indra. Although individuals are impermanent, the jāti is eternal.

Or words may denote a station, as does “prāḍvivāka.” A prāḍvivāka is not a particular person but a particular office; whoever occupies that office is called by that term. Likewise, Vedic words such as “Devāpi” may refer to a particular jāti or to a particular station. Therefore, it is said that Devāpi became a Brāhmaṇa through austerity in another birth and performed priestly service. Thus the account is connected with a Devāpi of another kalpa. “Let him do this”—in this way the whole meaning is explained by Durgācārya.

The interpretation that “rarāṇa” means rejoicing and being pleased through praise, recitation, meditation, and the like is contrary to the Nirukta. Durgācārya derives the word from “rāti,” meaning “giving,” and explains “rarāṇa” as “giving.”

Just as Viśvāmitra, in another kalpa, attained the state of a supreme seer through austerity, became a seer of mantras such as the Gāyatrī, and performed priestly service at sacrifices, so too did this Devāpi.

Attainment of jāti through austerity

Alternatively, that statement has this very meaning: Brāhmaṇa-hood is attained through a special form of austerity. For example, the perfected great sage Agastya, by the power of his words, caused Nahuṣa to become a python. This transformation took place through the dissolution of the particles that formed Nahuṣa’s body and the gathering of the particles that formed a python’s body. In the same way, for those perfected through extraordinary austerity, Brāhmaṇa-hood can be produced by dissolving the particles that form a Kṣatriya’s body and assembling the particles that form a Brāhmaṇa’s body. Priesthood in such a case is certainly possible. This is indeed known. But it does not follow that every Kṣatriya, even one unable to transform a mouse, can make himself a Brāhmaṇa through actions alone.

Changes of form without an intervening birth, through the extraordinary power of austerity and the force of weapons and the like, are exceptions: Nahuṣa attained divinity and then the state of a python; Ahalyā, Menakā, and others attained the state of stone and the like. This too is an exception due to the power of austerity. The general rule is that the arrangement of varṇas is by birth alone. The attainment of Brāhmaṇa-hood by Devāpi, Viśvāmitra, and others, even without an intervening birth, is an exception brought about by the extraordinary power of austerity.

Therefore, for those perfected by great austerity, or for the Lord who can create, undo, or transform, the transformation of material nature through a resolve expressed as a boon or a curse is not very difficult. Through Viśvāmitra’s austerity Menakā was transformed; the sons of Vasiṣṭha met their end; Triśaṅku was sent to heaven; and a new heaven was created.

It is then not difficult to produce Brāhmaṇa-hood by dissolving the particles that form a Kṣatriya body, in which rajas predominates and sattva is subordinate, and assembling the particles that form a Brāhmaṇa body, in which sattva and the power of the mouth predominate. Likewise, through austerity and resolve, the particles that form bodies of the other varṇas can be dissolved, and those that form a Brāhmaṇa body assembled. Just as an earthen pot cannot become a golden pot without the dissolution of the elements that formed the earlier pot and the assembly of the elements that form the new one, so too this should be understood in the present case.

The Gītā’s agreement

For this reason, the Gītā’s statement, “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me according to the division of guṇa and karma,” accords with this very view. Birth in the respective wombs is explained according to the divisions of guṇa and karma. Śruti establishes the attainment of wombs through good or bad conduct. Therefore, the Supreme Lord, the giver of the fruits of action, said that He created the fourfold varṇa of Brāhmaṇas and the others in accordance with the division of guṇa and karma.

It is not possible for just anyone to create this. Jāti is by birth, and birth follows guṇa and karma. Who but a madman would say otherwise?

Moreover, just as a pot is made from its material causes, such as clay, and not from something distinct from its material cause after the pot has come into being, so too, when the bodies of Brāhmaṇas and the others arise from atoms endowed with the specific powers that initiate those bodies, later guṇas and karmas distinct from those causes cannot be considered instrumental in bringing about that birth.

Later guṇas and karmas can certainly cause birth in a womb that corresponds to them in another existence. It is well known that actions and the qualities corresponding to them initiate jāti, lifespan, and experience. As it is said, “When the fruit of merit or demerit is intense, it is experienced in this very life.” In some cases, they may initiate another jāti in that same life; that is an exception.

Someone may object: “If such qualities and actions are not now present, how can their existence in a previous birth be known?” That is not so. Even if they are unknown to others, the Lord who gives the fruits of action knows them. It is impossible to perceive every action that initiates birth. Even so, their existence is not in doubt, since it can be inferred from its fruit. Likewise, the possibility of attaining a Brāhmaṇa’s or another varṇa’s womb does not contradict their existence.

Thus, the guṇas and karmas are the causes of birth in the wombs of Brāhmaṇas and the others, and through them the arrangement of varṇas follows. If the varṇa arrangement is accepted as depending on the guṇas and karmas manifested in the body after birth, then, as these change from day to day and moment to moment, varṇa too would change continually. There would then be no stable arrangement.

Varṇa by birth, excellence through action

An objection may be raised: If the qualities and actions proper to a Brāhmaṇa are the cause of that body, then only qualities and actions proper to a Brāhmaṇa should always be present in it, and contrary ones should not arise. That is not so, because their efficacy is exhausted in bringing about birth in that womb. In a suitable environment, through the rites performed by parents and others, instruction by a qualified teacher, study, understanding, conduct, and teaching, the qualities and actions proper to a Brāhmaṇa become manifest in the body. As the śruti says: “One who has a mother, a father, and a teacher.” Otherwise, the prescriptions for rites performed by parents and others would be pointless.

Thus, even when one has been born in a Brāhmaṇa womb through former good qualities and actions, because of inadequate care, heedlessness, bad company, and an adverse environment, the qualities such as calmness and self-restraint proper to a Brāhmaṇa are suppressed. Contrary qualities and actions arise, and in the end birth in a dog’s or pig’s womb may follow. In the same way, among Śūdras, the qualities and actions appropriate to them, arising from rajas predominating with tamas subordinate, may be overcome. Actions contrary to their own varṇa arise, and qualities proper to a Brāhmaṇa become manifest. Through these, in due course, attainment of Brāhmaṇa-hood and the like in another birth may occur. Therefore the conclusion is: varṇa is by birth; excellence is through action. Thus distinctions such as highest, middling, and lowest also fit.

This refutes the objection that the division of varṇa must be determined by the division of qualities and actions present at the time, since otherwise contradiction or a division of the sentence would result. The former actions that bring about jāti, lifespan, and experience have already accomplished their purpose. Once the result has been attained, the means need not be posited again; a cause does not become the cause of its own cause. When Brāhmaṇa-hood and the rest have already been established by qualities and actions from a previous birth, to posit present qualities and actions as necessary for that same result is to assume what is already established. If qualities and actions cause birth, and birth is the cause of the fourfold varṇa, then qualities and actions are causes of the fourfold varṇa as causes of its cause, like family descent and parents.

In the statement “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me,” the division of qualities and actions is stated as the instrumental cause in the creation of the fourfold varṇa. Creation here means birth; it is nothing other than that. Thus it is clear that the Lord produces the Brāhmaṇas and the rest by granting birth in their respective wombs in accordance with the division of qualities and actions. To accept an arrangement of varṇa based on qualities and actions alone, independently of birth, contradicts the statement that it was created by the Lord.

It is objected: “Even among those born in these respective wombs, the qualities and actions proper to each are not found; they are seen elsewhere as well, and there are many departures from the rule. Therefore it is not established that the division of the four varṇas follows only from the division of qualities and actions existing in a previous birth.” That is not so. Qualities and actions from a previous birth are not found in this body precisely because they belonged to a previous birth: the cause has been exhausted by producing the body. Even when good and bad bodies arise from good and bad actions, actions contrary to the nature of each body may arise. If the actions that produced the body alone brought about every subsequent good and bad action, human effort would be reduced to pouring water upon ashes, and the injunctions and prohibitions of scripture would be pointless. A heedless person acts on what is prohibited, ignoring the scripture of prohibition; one who is vigilant and attentive to scripture abandons what is prohibited and does what is prescribed.

Thus it is sometimes seen that, through former actions, one attains a womb and prosperity in wealth and grain, yet falls through heedlessness. Another may attain a low condition through former actions and, though reviled by many, rise to greatness through austerity. Rāvaṇa too, though he had attained great lordship through former austerities, is remembered as having fallen through heedlessness. “You have attained the fruit of dharma; of that there is no doubt. You will swiftly reap the fruit of this adharma as well.” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Sundara Kāṇḍa 7.51.22.)

The claim that creation is to be explained by qualities and actions manifest in the body is insubstantial, pointless, and contradictory. Were that so, the specific statement “The fourfold varṇa was created by Me” would be pointless, since the Lord is already the creator of all. Indeed, a distinction is recognized between what is created directly by the Lord and what is created by the Lord through beings who have assumed particular forms.

Moreover, the phrase “qualities and actions of a previous birth manifest in the present body” is pointless. If the intended meaning is that qualities and actions belonging to a previous birth are manifested in the present body, that is what should have been said; even then, it does not hold. Let the reasoning by a bad analogy be accepted: just as a pot made by a potter is called the Lord’s creation, so the fourfold varṇa made by someone else is called the Lord’s creation. Then one would have to abandon the force of the accepted means of knowledge and accept faith alone. The intermediate agent responsible here has not been specified. If it is society, society is nothing apart from an aggregate of individuals, and the aggregate is nothing apart from its individuals. Since individuals are limited in knowledge and lack the capacity to determine exceedingly subtle qualities and actions, the conclusion is indeterminacy. The foolish claim that some ignorant person or committee will determine the fourfold varṇa is mere prattle; no universally accepted assembly of learned and discerning scholars is established.

Defects in an arrangement of varṇa by action

Moreover, qualities and actions do not remain the same even within a single birth, because they change rapidly. Even one whose qualities and actions are good may, through heedlessness, become engaged in bad qualities and actions. Even one inclined toward bad qualities and actions may, through the company of the good and the force of human effort, attain excellence in qualities and actions. Examples of this are found in Vālmīki, Ajāmila, and others.

Because qualities change unevenly at every moment, these same distinctions are seen even through the divisions of the Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, and Kali ages. Just as the intervals between successive parts of a day are experienced, gradations are experienced for each person every day. If varṇa were determined by differences in qualities and actions, then varṇa would change from day to day. In the morning, when a person endowed with sattva performs meditation on and worship of the Supreme Lord, he would become a Brāhmaṇa. When he takes up a staff to drive away dogs and the like, he would become a Kṣatriya. When he engages in the buying and selling of salt and other goods, agriculture, and the care of cattle, he would become a Vaiśya. When he undertakes sweeping and cleaning the house, he would become a Śūdra. Such a state cannot be called an orderly arrangement.

Further, under such a theory, a Brāhmaṇa endowed at some time with tamasic qualities and actions would become a Śūdra, approach a Brāhmaṇa woman endowed with sattvic qualities, and beget a caṇḍāla in her. Out of fidelity, a wife cannot restrain even a badly behaved husband. A son endowed with sattvic qualities would regard his father, endowed with tamasic qualities, as a Śūdra and make him perform service. Thus, in a varṇa arrangement governed by qualities and actions, there would be utter disorder.

Qualities and actions carried over from another birth can manifest in their own form in a subsequent birth. Because they perish moment by moment, however, their earlier forms are not seen; their fruits, whose results have begun, are manifested through the inscrutable power of destiny, and the existence of the causes is inferred from the force of the fruit. “That which is inconceivable is destiny; it is not destroyed even among beings. Clearly, this reversal has fallen upon me and her. Who, O Saumitri, can undertake to fight destiny, when no action of it apart from its result can be seen?” (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa 2.22.20–21.) Such are the statements of the Rāmāyaṇa. Even when accumulated, those qualities and actions do not themselves become manifest, because, as the unseen and the like, they are beyond perception, inference, and scriptural testimony.

The meaning of svabhāva

The very accumulation of impressions produced by the repeated practice of action and knowledge is what is designated by words such as nature and inherent disposition. A student who has practiced those actions or fields of knowledge, and fallen asleep, on waking again engages in them because of the impressions produced by practice. In the same way, a being who has practiced those actions or knowledge, upon dying and reaching another birth, engages there in them through that very former practice.

“By that very former practice he is carried on, even though helpless.” (Bhagavad Gītā 6.44.) “Even a man of knowledge acts according to his own nature; beings follow their nature. What will restraint accomplish?” (Bhagavad Gītā 3.33.) “Bound by your own action born of nature, O son of Kuntī, what through delusion you do not wish to do, you will do helplessly.” (Bhagavad Gītā 18.60.)

Elsewhere, birth and death are said to be like sleeping and waking. “When, through absorption in sense objects, a living being no longer remembers itself, that complete forgetfulness, whatever its cause, is called death. Birth, O bounteous one, is a person’s identification with all things as the self; acceptance of sense objects is said to be like a dream or a desire.” (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 11.22.38–39.)
