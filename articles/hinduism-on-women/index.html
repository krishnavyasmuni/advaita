1. Introduction

I. Preface

Purpose

Dharma is one of the most difficult Sanskrit words to translate into English. It refers simultaneously to the innate qualities and to the appropriate duties, It is the ideal way that a thing ought to exist. Insofar as dharma is often discussed, however, it is discussed in the context of the dharmas of specific Varṇas, Jatis, 4Sramas, roles such as ‘king’, ‘teacher’, ‘student’, etc, and the chief object of discussion is the men whom these categories divide. Rarely is the dharma of women, half of the population, ever discussed on its own.

Even when it is discussed, it is discussed mostly in two ways. Firstly, from the lens of Indology, a completely secular and outsider perspective, which seeks to understand Hinduism as a naturally developing social structure which changed and evolved based on human factors. Secondly is through the completely ‘Traditionalist’ perspective, which, though recognising scripture as divine in premise, is practically more focused on auxiliary social practices, ideas of later thinkers and even internalised foreign influence. Rarely, if ever, is the actual statement of the Hindu scriptures themselves engaged on its own.

This paper attempts to do just that, understand the position of the Hindu scriptures themselves, without seeking to project ideas of ‘Later’ or ‘older’ on them, without contextualising them as mere human productions based on human factors , without mixing in external practices and influences, completely on its own terms as divine pramana.

Methodology

As stated, only the statement of scriptures will be considered primary evidence for this analysis. Scripture includes Sruti or Vedas as the supreme section, containing 4 sub-sections,viz, Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa, Aranyaka & Upaniṣad. These very rarely speak of social laws. Besides Sruti is the wide category of ‘Smṛti’, the writings of the authoritative sages. Contained within are many sub-categories like Vedangas, Dharmasastras (Social-legal treatises), Itihasas (History) and Purāṇas (which focus on a broad range of topics and are sometimes considered distinct from smrtis), and a few others. 3. Marriage

I. Introduction

Importance of marriage

The most important legal and social institution tied to women (and men) is perhaps marriage. Marriage is for this reason its own section in this document. Marriage in Hinduism is not merely a social contract, but a divine obligation with few exceptions for both women and men. The Manusmiti (9.96) describes it as the very purpose of both:

Mahimā of the Gṛhastha āśrama

The rna to the sages is paid by studying as a student (Brahmacarya), but for the remaining two, i.e , to procreate and perform religious activities, marriage is obligatory. Thus after Brahmacarya one should marry and enter the ‘Grhastha’ (Household) phase of life, and then procreate and perform religious activities , fulfilling the first three goals of life [ Righteousness (Dharma), Sensual pleasure (Kama), wealth (Artha) ] and the duties of his Varṇa until old age, only then should one take renunciation (Vanaprastha and eventually Samnyasa) and focus entirely on attaining on the final goal, liberation (Mokṣa).

This, in summary, is the entirety of human Dharma, and has marriage at its basis. Without marrying and entering Grhastha life one cannot fulfil two of the three rnas, cannot progress in the asramas, cannot perform the duties of his varṇa and cannot fulfill even a single one of the Purusarthas.

On Rākṣasa marriage

The most controversial form of Marriage is perhaps the Raksasa, not just amongst people but even in the scriptures. The other Smrtis and Śāstras may contain content pertaining to other yuga, but the Parasara smṛti, itself stated to be meant specifically for Kaliyuga, cannot contain any injunctions “ pertaining to other Yugas” (Yugantaravisayah) as claimed by Vidyaranya and others. On purdah

e On “purdah”

A very commonly accepted idea amongst Hindus, especially in the north and especially in the rural areas, is that women ought to cover their head, or even their face. One may see women of the house in the north rush to drape the ghunghat over their hair and face when a man other than their husband or blood relatives enters the home, or put their Saree’s dupatta over their hair when entering a temple. Even in the shows or films adapting stories from Hindu scriptures, all the good women cover their head, and only the demonic women are shown without such covering.

One may notice, however, that the śāstras enjoin women to cover their lower body from the navel down to slightly above the ankles, and their chest, but nothing at all is stated about covering the head or face. In fact, the numerous head and face ornaments shown just above, would all become invisible and thereby useless, if the head or face were covered.

If this practice is not in the scriptures, then where has it come from? Both the word ‘Purdah’, which is a Persian word, and its geographic prevalence, the north of India, which was occupied by Muslim powers for far longer than the south, give us the answer. It is a borrowing of a muslim concept, and not a Hindu Scriptural one. Historian A.S. Altekar writes in his book, ‘the position of women in hindu civilization’ , pages 206-207:

“There are absolutely no traces of any Purdah observed within the family in the Hindu period. The general adoption of the Purdah system by the ruling and aristocratic families of Hindu community is subsequent to the advent of the Muslim rule. It was accepted by Hindu society in imitation of the manners of the conquerors.”

Further he states:

“This happened almost universally in northern India, where the Muslim rule and culture were in ascendancy for a long time. In the Deccan, the Muslim influence was superficial, and so the Purdah system got no footing in the Hindu society there ”

Not only is the practice itself absent from Hindu scriptures and pre-islamic Hindu society as a whole, but even the rationale for it is explicitly rejected. In Islam, purdah is done to protect the women of the house from the male gaze. However, lord Rama himself states :

Valmiki Ramayana 6.117.23-4

a Tere 4 aeofer 4 Tea Pha [AGA AGTAART FAATALT FEAT: Ih BAA FA POY A Fay aaa 14 shalt at flare ar aaa goa fSrar: 1

“ Neither the house nor clothes nor walls nor veils/curtains nor royal protection truly protects the woman, but only her character. Whether in times of crisis, in war, in a choosing ceremony, in religious events or in weddings, does public appearance ever pollute a woman.”

This sentiment is echoed in non-religious literature also. In the kathasaritasagara, 7.2, a character named Ratnaprabha, is told by her husband to stay within the house. She tells him:

“ ‘By staying in the inner quarters of the house there is protection’, this I do not accept. Only by the greatness of their good character are the women of the house protected.”

Thus, the practice of purdah, viz, compulsorily covering anything other than the lower body from navel to ankle and the chest, and general seclusion inside the home, is entirely absent from Hindu scriptures and ancient Hindu society, and even its base rationale explicitly rejected.

V. Education

Education in the Vedas

The general restriction of women from Vedic study

The discussion of women's education in the Hindu scriptures must be separated into two two separate discussions, (i) Education in the Vedas, and (ii) Education in all the topics other than the Vedas, for indeed these are very different.

The Vedas are the most sacred scriptures of Hinduism. They are not a book that is bought and read, rather they are only heard (hence, also called Sruti, “that which is heard” ) from a guru. This hearing is not simple either, it begins with the initiation with the sacred thread ceremony, which is only performed for the first three Varṇas and not the Sidra, after which the child goes to live with the Guru and study under him, serving him, and has to follow hundreds if not thousands of rules while doing so. During this time he must beg for alms (Bhiksatana). Only then may he learn the Vedic mantras and their use in Yajfias (Fire rituals) from the guru.

In the case of general women, however, the Manusmnrti(2.67) states:

“For women, marriage is equivalent to the Vedic initiation rite, serving the husband equivalent to living with (and serving) the guru, and the performance of household duties equivalent to the performance of Vedic fire rituals.”

Therefore women in general are not enjoined to study the Vedas. It is for them (and the Sidras and uninitiated) that the other Vedic literatures, the itihasas and purāṇas, called the fifth Veda, have been composed.

4. Addendum

I. Conclusion

Thus, almost every topic of a woman’s life, legal, social and religious, has been explained, with the scripture as the primary divine guide without any historicisation, and with the Acaryas as supporting evidence. All the topics discussed may now be summarised by chronology in the life of a woman.

When she is born, one ought to rejoice, for Devi herself has come to his home, and the daughter is equal or superior to the son. Her samskaras should be performed, and she should be given education. If she is an exceptional in religious topics, her sacred thread initiation should be done and she taught the Vedas as well, though not otherwise. When she reaches the appropriate age, she should be married in one of the higher forms of marriage, without exchange of money and with her consent, to a suitable husband. If a suitable husband is not found, she may remain unmarried, though marriage is superior to pay the rnas and enter the grhastha stage.

When she is married, she should be loved, served, provided for and protected by her husband, for whom she is the supreme refuge. She too should serve him, perform the household work like cooking, cleaning, etc, and advise him on matters of family and business. When she has children she is more venerable to them than their father or teacher. If her husband has gone away for a long time, or is impotent, or has taken renunciation, or has fallen, then she may leave him and remarry. If he dies she may live as a widow or remarry, she should not perform sahagamana.

Whatever money she receives is her own and may not to be taken by anyone, or else they will be legally punished. If her husband has gone away without making arrangements for her she may work in respectable industries.

She receives inheritance from her father if there are no sons, or she is unmarried. She receives inheritance from her husband, equal to a son, or the entirety of it if there are no children. Upon her own death her property is inherited by her daughters, sons if no daughters or husband/parents depending on type of marriage.

She is to be guarded—not dominated— by her father in childhood, husband in youth and sons in old age. If not, by the government/king. She is inviolable. Any verbal, physical or sexual offence against her is a more serious crime than against a man. Abandonment and adultery are punishable offenses.

She should wear clothing such that it covers her chest, and lower body from navel to ankle. If she goes out, she should take an uttariya over arms and/or shoulder. All else is optional. She should wear the marital ornaments always, and other ornaments as per her wish. There is no fault in her going out in public.

Menstruation is the unparalleled means of purification for the women, like the flow of the river. Though it leaves them temporarily impure during that time, and they should cease religious household activities during it. At childbirth both she and her husband are impure for ten days. She should maintain internal purity by meditating on God with her husband, and external by bathing.

She may perform religious activities like fasting, pilgrimage, chanting, making offerings, charity, etc. though fulfilling her household duties itself is also sufficient. By performing bhakti of her preferred deity she attains Mokṣa.

Some may call the scriptural views ‘Progressive’, though this is a very bad label, because progress implies gradual improvement from a less than ideal state. The scriptures, however, are eternal. The only issue is the misinterpretation and addition. Thus what is to be done is not ‘progress’ but ‘purification’ to arrive at the original scriptural view.
