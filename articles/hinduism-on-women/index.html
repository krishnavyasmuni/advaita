1. Introduction

I. Preface

Purpose

Dharma is one of the most difficult Sanskrit words to translate into English. It refers simultaneously to innate qualities and appropriate duties; it is the ideal way that a thing ought to exist. Insofar as dharma is often discussed, however, it is discussed in the context of the dharmas of specific Varṇas, Jātis, Āśramas, roles such as “king,” “teacher,” and “student,” and the chief object of discussion is the men whom these categories divide. Rarely is the dharma of women, half of the population, ever discussed on its own.

Even when it is discussed, it is discussed mostly in two ways. Firstly, from the lens of Indology, a completely secular and outsider perspective, which seeks to understand Hinduism as a naturally developing social structure which changed and evolved based on human factors. Secondly is through the completely ‘Traditionalist’ perspective, which, though recognising scripture as divine in premise, is practically more focused on auxiliary social practices, ideas of later thinkers and even internalised foreign influence. Rarely, if ever, is the actual statement of the Hindu scriptures themselves engaged on its own.

This paper attempts to do just that: understand the position of the Hindu scriptures themselves, without seeking to project ideas of “later” or “older” on them, without contextualising them as mere human productions based on human factors, and without mixing in external practices and influences, entirely on their own terms as divine pramāṇa.

Methodology

As stated, only the statement of scriptures will be considered primary evidence for this analysis. Scripture includes Śruti or Vedas as the supreme section, containing four sub-sections, viz., Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa, Āraṇyaka, and Upaniṣad. These very rarely speak of social laws. Besides Śruti is the wide category of Smṛti, the writings of the authoritative sages. Contained within are many sub-categories like Vedāṅgas, Dharmaśāstras (social-legal treatises), Itihāsas (history), and Purāṇas (which focus on a broad range of topics and are sometimes considered distinct from Smṛtis), and a few others. It is these alone which are sources of Dharma, as they themselves state:

Lord Kṛṣṇa says in the Gītā, 16.23 (part of the itihasa Mahābhārata)

" He who, discarding scriptural procedure, proceeds as per his own wishes, he does not attain success, neither happiness, nor the highest goal. Therefore, the scripture is your basis in the determination of what is to be done and what is not to be done. Having known the scriptural injunction, it befits you to perform actions in this world."

Vasiṣṭha smṛti 1.2

states :

“ That which is enjoined by Sruti or smṛti is dharma”

Bhagavata Purāṇa 6.1.40

states:

“ That which is enjoined in Vedic scriptures is dharma and what is other than it is adharma. ”

There is also a hierarchy within these scriptures (Vyasa smṛti 1.4):

“when there is contradiction between Sruti, Smṛti & Purāṇa, Sruti (alone) shall be pramana. And between the two (Smṛti and Purāṇa), the Smṛti is superior. ”

However this hierarchy should only be invoked when all possible reconciliation has been ruled out.

In order to understand the scriptures , logic (Nyaya) and Hermeneutics (Mimamsa) are also needed. The opinion of learned scholars (Acaryas) are also considered, but only insofar as they are logical and based on the scripture, as the scriptures themselves say:

(Yoga vasiṣṭha written by valmiki, (2.18.3) )

“That which is reasonable should be accepted even if it be stated by a child, and if it is not so, it should be given up like a piece of straw, even if it be stated by the lotus-born god brahma himself”

"One should accept ( of the guru) what is reasonable or established

(by scripture), he should not accept it merely by it having been stated by the guru.

Hence, even the statements and actions of Acharyas are subject to the authority of scriptures and are to be judged and discarded if they are not in line with them. Only scriptures are authority in their own right.

Names of women

Sanskrit is one of the richest languages in terms of synonyms for a single thing. A synonym is not random but based on a specific quality of the thing it denotes. Fire, for example, while usually called “Agni,” is also called “Pāvaka” (purifier), as whatever is put in it is burnt and thereby purified.

The Amarakoṣa is a very famous lexicon which records such synonyms for each thing, arranged by topic in metrical verses. While not itself a scripture, its words almost always have examples of scriptural use.

For “Men” it contains only two lines, of mostly generic names deriving from “descendant of Manu” (Mānava, Mānuṣa, Manuṣya, Manuja). The women, however, get five lines of highly descriptive names, a testament to how many qualities of women are recognised in scriptures. The following are a few select examples of the names it records:

  1. Strī: she who bears the womb.
  2. Nārī: she who leads.

  5. Yoṣit: she who is served by men.
  6. Yoṣā: she who serves men.
  7. Vāmā: she who embodies beauty.
  8. Rāmā: delighted or one who delights others.
  9. Māninī: she who has dignity.

As mothers and daughters

Women, of course , do not exist in a vacuum. They are related to others as mothers, daughters, sisters, etc. of all relations, the one which everyone has and which is the root of all others, is that of the mother. The mother too is heavily glorified in the scriptures:

"In venerability, the teacher excels ten Sub-teachers; the Father a hundred teachers, and the Mother a thousand Fathers.”

A famous section of Mahābhārata is the Yaksa-prasna, a series of riddles by a creature called Yaksa to Yudhiṣṭhira, who is dharma incarnate, with the lives of his brothers on the line. A riddle in it is:

3. Marriage

I. Introduction

Importance of marriage

The most important legal and social institution tied to women (and men) is perhaps marriage. Marriage is for this reason its own section in this document. Marriage in Hinduism is not merely a social contract, but a divine obligation with few exceptions for both women and men. The Manusmiti (9.96) describes it as the very purpose of both:

"Women were created for the purpose of child-bearing, and men for the purpose of procreation. Hence it is that Religious Rites have been ordained in the Veda as common between the man and his wife.”

To understand why marriage is considered so important, one must understand the three debts. The Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda ( 6.3.10.5) amongst various other sources, states:

“ One is born with three debts, of learning to the sages, of Vedic sacrifice to the gods, and of progeny to the ancestors.”

Similarly Manusmṛti(6:36) :

“Only after having studied the Vedas according to the rules, having begotten sons in the rightful manner, and having offered sacrifices to the rest of his ability, he shall turn his mind towards Liberation.”

Disqualification of the unmarried from religious observances

The second rna, to the gods, is paid by religious observances. However, as stated, religious observances have been stated to be common between the husband and wife, and so neither can perform them without the other :

Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 3.3.3.1

" He is without the Vedic ritual , who is without a wife."

Skanda purāṇa 4.1.4.68; Śiva purāṇa 2.6.54.66

“The man without a wife does not have the right to the rites of the gods, ancestors, guests, or Vedic rituals.”

Skanda Purāṇa 4.1.4.19; Śiva Purāṇa 2.6.54.19

" A Woman must not perform religious observances such as fasting without the prior knowledge of her husband"

Mahimā of the Gṛhastha āśrama

The rna to the sages is paid by studying as a student (Brahmacarya), but for the remaining two, i.e , to procreate and perform religious activities, marriage is obligatory. Thus after Brahmacarya one should marry and enter the ‘Grhastha’ (Household) phase of life, and then procreate and perform religious activities , fulfilling the first three goals of life [ Righteousness (Dharma), Sensual pleasure (Kama), wealth (Artha) ] and the duties of his Varṇa until old age, only then should one take renunciation (Vanaprastha and eventually Samnyasa) and focus entirely on attaining on the final goal, liberation (Mokṣa).

This, in summary, is the entirety of human Dharma, and has marriage at its basis. Without marrying and entering Grhastha life one cannot fulfil two of the three rnas, cannot progress in the asramas, cannot perform the duties of his varṇa and cannot fulfill even a single one of the Purusarthas.

It is thus that married life is praised heavily in scriptures:

Manusmṛti 6.86-87

" Brahmacari ( student), Grhastha (householder), Vanaprastha and Samnyasa , these four separate asramas have the Grhastha as the basis. As per the injunctions of the Veda and Smṛti, amongst all of these, the Grhastha is said to be supreme, for he sustains the other three ”

Mahābhārata 12.261.7

austerities. Married life is the basis of all of dharma that proceeds.

Padma purāṇa 2.59.16-17

" There is no superior dharma separate to married life on this earth. The householder's home is said to be endowed with purity and truth."

On consent

Regarding the marriage of the women, the Manusmṛti & Mahābhārata state :

“Having reached her puberty, the righteous maiden shall wait for three years, with the beginning of the fourth, she may choose a suitable husband for herself”

Answer: not so, for the same chapter of the Mahābhārata states a few verses ahead:

“Manu does not praise the co-habitation of the girl with whom she does not desire.”

The fifth type of Marriage, Gāndharva, which is characterized entirely by the mutual love and desire of the bride and groom and no involvement of the parents, is highly praised in scriptures and will be discussed shortly.

II. Types of Marriage

The eight types

The eight types of marriage are summarised in short in the Garuḍa Purāṇa (1.95.7–10):

“(I) The marriage in which the father invites a man endowed with good qualities and lineage to receive his daughter’s hand is called Brāhma.”

“(II) That in which the daughter’s hand is given to an officiating priest is called Daiva.”

“(III) The one in which a pair of cow and bull is accepted as a symbolic gift is called Ārṣa.”

“(IV) The one in which the groom himself asks for the daughter’s hand and is given it is called Prājāpatya, which is a righteous form.”

“(V) The one in which there is an exchange of money is called Āsura, an inferior form.”

“(VI) The one which is done with the mutual agreement of the bride and groom, and none else, is called Gāndharva.”

“(VII) The one in which martial force is used by the groom is called Rākṣasa.”

“(VIII) The one where the girl is deceived or cheated by being intoxicated, asleep, or lied to is called Paiśāca.”

Asura marriage and dowry

Amongst these, some are righteous whereas others are unrighteous. The classification given in Nārada Smṛti (12.44) is as follows :

" Amongst these, the first four starting with brahma are dharmika (righteous). The Gāndharva form is common to all, and all the subsequent three are Adharmika ( unrighteous). ”

Asura, the form characterised by exchange of money, is considered adharmic. It should be noted that the exchange referred to here is not The modern practice of dowry prevalent in India, where the bride’s family pays a certain amount in money or gifts to the groom’s family. That is entirely a medieval innovation with no basis in the scriptures. What the scriptures do mention is the exact opposite practice, where the groom pays a certain amount to the bride’s family. This is condemned in Manusmṛti (9.98):

“ By accepting dowry for the girl what he (the father) does is nothing but the covert sale of a daughter. This practice has never been performed by good men, neither of the present nor past.”

The modern practice, too, is thereby forbidden, firstly because of its complete lack of scriptural injunction (as seen, only the scriptures are the source of dharma) and by analogy, as what is condemned here is the sale of a child, whether it be of a daughter as done earlier or of a son as in modern times.

Greatness of the Gandharva form

Generally, the Brahma form is considered the best amongst the eight types. However , it is limited to only the Brāhmaṇa varṇa. The Gāndharva form, where the bride and groom marry out of mutual love only without the involvement of the parents, is considered ‘Sadharana’ (may be practiced by all varṇas).

In modern times, such a marriage which occurs only by the wish of the bride and groom and none else, will be termed ‘love marriage’ and considered ‘Un-traditional’ The scriptures, however, contain much praise for it, even as the best form:

Mahābhārata 1.67.4

" Amongst the forms of marriages, O you with beautiful thighs, , the Gāndharva is said to be the best "

“Harita has stated that for all Varṇas, there is superiority in the Gāndharva type.”

An example of Gāndharva marriage is that of King duḥṣanta , father of King Bharata, after whom the Mahābhārata and the country of India itself (Bharata) is named, and Śakuntalā (Mahābhārata 1.67):

“O auspicious one of great form! From how you speak, you appear to be a princess. Become my wife, O beautiful one; tell me what your conditions are.”

Śakuntalā hesitates, thinking she should wait for her father’s order:

“My father, O king, who eats only fruit, has gone out of the residence. Wait just a moment, he shall give my hand to you.”

“One is one’s own friend and one is one’s own refuge. You may give your own self in marriage righteously. As I am desirous of you and you of me, O excellent-complexioned, you can become my wife by Gāndharva marriage.”

Convinced, Śakuntalā states her terms for marriage :

“Tf this is the way of dharma, If Iam my own master in the matter of giving in marriage, O best of the Puru lineage, then hear my terms. The son born to me shall become king after you, O king, promise me this truthfully."

When her father, Kanva, returns, he also approves of the marriage :

“What you have done today, viz, marrying a man in the manner fit for royalty, is not a violation of Dharma.”

Thus, Dharma gives women complete agency, both to choose their husband (Atma prabhur pradane) and to do so on their own terms which a suitor must accept to marry them.

On Rākṣasa marriage

The most controversial form of Marriage is perhaps the Raksasa, not just amongst people but even in the scriptures. As seen, The Nārada Smṛti completely disapproves of it, classifying it alongside Asura and Paisaca as adharma. The Manusmṛti, however, permits it for Kṣatriyas ( ‘rata atfrarey’ , 3.24). Mahābhārata lies somewhere in the middle, permitting it for kings but not ordinary Kṣatriyas ( ‘aig waratsepeAY 1.67.11). Some however, object to this, as they mistakenly consider the “martial force” which characterises the Raksasa marriage to be force on the bride against her consent. This is, however, not so, as the commentators on Manu clarify :

“Taking the girl by using force against the family of the girl is called the Rākṣasa marriage.”

“Using martial force against the girl’s family if they come in opposition is the meaning .”

Thus the Raksasa vivaha is a form performed by Kṣatriyas where, if the girl’s family opposes the marriage, the girl being ready for it, then the Kṣatriya, in a display of valour, battles the girl’s family and takes her by force against them.

Nārada smṛti (12.98-100)

(ID Has died : self-explanatory

Remarriage

(III) Has taken renunciation: the husband is generally substantially older than the wife. If he has reached the time for renunciation and wishes to do so, and the wife does not, she may remarry after he renounces.

(IV) If he is impotent : as stated, the very purpose of marriage is primarily procreation. If the husband doesn't fulfill his sexual duties, or if he is infertile, the woman may annul the marriage and remarry.

(V) If he has fallen : “fallen” (Patita) is a technical term in Dharmasastra meaning one who has committed a major sin ( Pataka). In such cases too she may nullify and remarry.

Objection : this practice is Kali-varjya, as argued by Acarya Vidyaranya in his commentary on the ParaSara smṛti on this verse :

“ This matter of remarriage is of a separate yuga ( and not this one) , as stated in the Aditya-purāṇa : “the remarriage of the married woman, the elder son’s extra share of property, sacrifice of cows, niyoga with the brother-in-law and use of the kamandalu, these five are not to be done in kaliyuga.”

(This verse has also been quoted by Hemadri’s Caturvargacintamani, Mitra misra’s Viramitrodaya , Smrtimuktavali of Krsnacarya and other works of acaryas, though it is absent from any extant Aditya-purāṇa)

Answer : the ParaSara smṛti itself states in its very beginning (1.24):

“ The Manusmnti is for the Krtayuga, the Gautama for the treta, the Sankhalikhita for the dvapara and the Paragara is for the Kaliyuga.”

Widowhood

The other Smrtis and Śāstras may contain content pertaining to other yuga, but the Parasara smṛti, itself stated to be meant specifically for Kaliyuga, cannot contain any injunctions “ pertaining to other Yugas” (Yugantaravisayah) as claimed by Vidyaranya and others. If a Purāṇa claims anything enjoined by it to be forbidden in Kaliyuga, it would be in direct contradiction with the smṛti itself, and must be rejected.

Thereby, to sustain the pramanya of the Aditya purāṇa verse, a reconciliatory approach must be taken. ‘Marriage of one who is already married’ may plausibly be interpreted as referring to the practice of a woman marrying another man while her previous marriage remains valid, i.e polyandry. This was indeed practiced in previous yugas, the most famous example being Draupadi in the dvapara yuga, and is now forbidden in kaliyuga.

Note that, the dissolution of the previous marriage in these 5 cases is mandatory before pursuing another marriage. This is stated not in this verse but in another parallel verse in the Devala smṛti (1548):

“A husband who is lost, has taken samnyasa, is impotent, fallen, a traitor or has died, is to be denounced by the wife.”

Right after the verse in the Parasara smṛti allowing for remarriage of a widow, it speaks of those women who do not wish to remarry. For them it states (4.31)

“ The wife who, upon the death of her husband, takes a vow of celibacy, she attains heaven upon death, like other celibates.”

Sati

The chapter of Parāśara concludes with one final option for the widow (4.32):

“For thirty-five million years, or for as many years as there are hairs on the human body, she stays in heaven who follows her husband in death.”

This practice of following the husband in death and being burnt on the same pyre as him is popularly called Sati and, more properly, Sahagamana. Similar injunctions are found in various other Purāṇas and Smṛtis. Vijñāneśvara also quotes this verse from the Śaṅkha and Aṅgiras Smṛtis in his commentary on Yājñavalkya Smṛti 1.86. Note that Sati is given as the last option: first she is told to remarry; if she does not wish to do so, to live as a widow; and only finally is Sahagamana offered. The mainstream traditional position accepts Sati as a Kāmya (desirable) act, but not even the most conservative Ācāryas consider it obligatory (Naimittika).

There is, however, a minority opinion among traditional Ācāryas that considers the act entirely forbidden (Niṣiddha). For example, Medhātithi argues in his commentary on Manu 5.157:

“Suicide is forbidden for women, as it is for men. Regarding the Smṛti text that ‘she shall die after her husband,’ this also is not an obligatory act that must be done. It is connected with a specific mention of a fruit, and is only to be done by someone desirous of that fruit; therefore, it is comparable to the Śyena sacrifice. Just as the Vedas say of it, ‘He shall kill beings by performing the Śyena,’ making its performance possible, but since it is only to be performed by one blinded by hatred, its performance cannot be stated to be dharma.”

“Similarly here, if a woman, desirous of the mentioned fruit, violates the prohibition on suicide and performs Sahagamana, this cannot be said to be enjoined by scripture. Thus the act of killing herself after her husband is forbidden for the woman. There is also the Vedic text ‘one shall not die till his lifetime has run out’; a Smṛti which violates an extant Śruti should be assumed to have some other meaning.”

Medhātithi makes a multilayered argument. First, the example of the Śyena sacrifice—which is mentioned in the Vedas—is considered forbidden according to the Mīmāṃsā sūtras, because it is enjoined for one who desires to kill, and desiring to kill is itself adharma. Therefore, the performance of the rite is adharma. Similarly, although Sahagamana and its fruit are mentioned in the Śāstra, it can only be performed by one who desires to attain heaven by suicide; since suicide itself is immoral, the practice is still adharma even though it is mentioned by the Śāstra.

Alternatively, he cites the Śruti “He shall not die before his lifespan runs out” from the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 10.2.6.7. Another Śruti forbidding suicide is Īśa Upaniṣad 3:

“Those realms are called sunless; they are filled with darkness. There go, after passing away, those who killed themselves.”

Since these extant Śrutis clearly forbid suicide, Smṛti, which derives its prāmāṇya from an inferred basis in lost Śruti, cannot contradict it. If they appear to contradict it, such as by enjoining suicide for a woman if her husband dies, they must be either reinterpreted or considered apramāṇa and rejected.

Before invoking the hierarchy and rejecting any lower pramāṇa, reconciliation must be ruled out. The mainstream Ācāryas have indeed attempted to reconcile this. Aparārka, for example, writes in his commentary on Yājñavalkya Smṛti 1.87:

“If it is objected that this is forbidden by the Śruti ‘He shall live out his full life,’ this is explained by treating that as a general rule and the Smṛti of ascending the funeral pyre as a specific rule. The general injunction prohibits suicide except where there is a specific injunction. A Smṛti that differs from a general Śruti cannot be considered invalid, because they differ in scope—one general and one specific—and thus there is no contradiction.”

Tryambaka, in his Strīdharmapaddhati, adds the example of animal sacrifice: one Śruti forbids killing any being, whereas another enjoins it in certain rites. The two are recognised as a general rule (Sāmānya or Utsarga) and a specific exception (Viśeṣa or Apavāda).

This, however, has one obvious flaw. By the definition of general and exceptional injunctions, the exceptional is stronger than the general. In the example given, both injunctions were from the Vedas themselves, so that does not matter.

However, in this case, the general injunction is a Vedic injunction and the exception would have to be from Smṛti; that is, Smṛti would have to be considered stronger than Śruti here. Even Aparārka acknowledges this, stating: “The specific Smṛti is stronger than the general Śruti and thereby overrides it.” For a Smṛti to be considered stronger than or to override a Śruti is, in any case, an utter absurdity. That is why the Viṣaya-bheda reconciliation is suggested for internal contradiction between Smṛtis, not between Śruti and Smṛti.

Devī Bhāgavata 11.1.22–23 states:

“Upon contradiction between the three (Śruti, Smṛti, and Purāṇa), Śruti is supreme. Between the two, Smṛti is superior. When two Śrutis conflict, both should be considered true, optionally. When two Smṛtis conflict, one should consider them to be of different Viṣayas (general and exceptional).”

The other side also argues that Smṛti may act as an exception to Śruti because the authority of Smṛtis is based on assuming their basis in a lost Śruti. Thus, they say, it would be that lost Śruti acting as the exception. Medhātithi addresses this reasoning while commenting on Manu 9.6:

“In the case of two extant Śrutis, they are reconciled by considering optional application, because they are of equal strength. Here, in the case of Smṛtis, however, the Vedic basis is merely assumed.”

“There is no prospect of equal conflict between the extant and the assumed, because the Vedic basis is merely a possibility and not a certainty, whereas the extant Śruti is certain. Never does the uncertain act as the exception (Bādhyate) to the certain.”

Therefore, though it is the minority position, the author argues that the stronger position is that Sati or Sahagamana is Niṣiddha, or forbidden.

Objection: it is seen in the Itihāsa-Purāṇas that many great women performed Sahagamana, for example some wives of Kṛṣṇa:

Mahābhārata 16.8.71–72

“Rukmiṇī, Gāndhārī, Śaibyā, Haimavatī, and goddess Jāmbavatī—all these wives of Kṛṣṇa entered the funeral pyre. Satyabhāmā and other goddesses, beloved of Kṛṣṇa, entered the forest, O king, intent on performing austerities.”

Answer: the primary authority for determining what is to be done and not to be done can only be direct injunction, not the actions of great personalities. Āpastamba Dharmasūtra 2.13.7–9 says:

“Transgressions of dharma and bold acts are seen among men of the past. Due to their distinguished power, they incur no fault from this; but a later person who imitates them perishes (that is, falls into sin).”

Similarly, Gautama Dharmasūtra 1.1.3–4 states:

“Transgression of dharma and bold acts are seen among great men (but these are not to be followed) because of the relative weakness of ordinary people.”

Thus, great personalities of the past—Ṛṣis, Avatāras, Devas, and others—are seen to perform acts contrary to ordinary dharma, yet they incur no fault because ordinary dharma does not bind them due to their high status. If someone imitates them, he falls into sin. Their injunctions are therefore to be followed, but not always their actions.

In this case, other Avatāras such as Lakṣmaṇa are also seen to have given up their bodies by immersing themselves in water, and the wives of Kṛṣṇa did so by entering the funeral pyre. Neither case is to be imitated. They are part of that Avatāra’s Līlā and, if done by ordinary people, are suicide.

To summarise, Sahagamana (Sati) is given as a final option in the Smṛtis like Parāśara after remarriage and widowhood, and it is considered a voluntary permissible act by the majority of Ācāryas. A minority argue that it is forbidden, analogous to the Śyena sacrifice, due to explicit prohibitions on suicide in the Vedas. The other side attempts to reconcile this by considering the Vedic passage a general statement and the Smṛti passages exceptions. However, this is not tenable because an exception is stronger than the general statement, but Smṛtis can never be stronger than Śrutis, as the uncertain cannot be an exception for the certain. The argument from venerable figures in the Itihāsas performing Sahagamana is further refuted by passages from Gautama and Āpastamba stating that such figures of the past are not to be emulated as dharma did not fully bind them due to their distinguished power. Thus Sahagamana being forbidden is the stronger position.

I. Property & Inheritance

One of the most frequently misinterpreted verses on women is the following verse from Manusmirti (9.3):

The issue is that people see only the last phrase and completely ignore that the preceding three phrases of the verse say “Shall protect”, Not “shall rule” or any such thing. In that context the word "Independence" (41d=4) refers to her being left without protection. This was conveyed by the verse of the Garuda purāṇa we saw earlier as well, which was almost the same except it ended with “and otherwise by other relatives”. The sense being that, failing the designated guardians, someone else should guard her, and she should not be left unprotected. This is stated by Medhatithi in his commentary as well:

“ By this it is not enjoined that she is not to be independent in any matter whatsoever, rather she isn't to be independent for her own protection as she lacks the physical strength to do so herself.”

As an example of matters in which she is independent, we come to the topic of Strīdhana.

Strīdhana

As stated, women are assigned to account for and spend the general funds of the house, provided by their husband. They also possess their own separate property, called Strīdhana, defined as:

Yājñavalkya Smṛti 2.146

“What is given by the father, mother, husband, or brothers; what is given during the marriage by other relations; what is given as compensation on supersession; and so on, is called Strīdhana.”

Regarding “and so on” (ādi), Vijñāneśvara comments:

“‘And so on’ refers to inheritance, purchase, partition, and whatever else she finds or is given.”

All this is considered the property of the woman, over which she has complete rights. The Kātyāyana Smṛti (906) states:

“Women’s independence with regard to their own property has always been stated, even to sell or give as they please, and even on immovable assets.”

For the male relatives to violate the sanctity of the woman’s right to her own property has both spiritual and legal consequences:

Manusmṛti 3.52

“Those relations who, through folly, live upon the Strīdhana, such as the bride’s conveyances and clothes, are sinners and fall into the lowest state.”

Kātyāyana Smṛti 911–912

“Neither husband, nor son, nor father, nor brothers have authority to take or dispose of the Strīdhana. If any of them partake of it by force, they shall be punished and be liable to pay the sum with interest.”

On women working

The Hindu scriptures, though equally honouring the husband and wife, nevertheless recognise their roles as different and complementary. The husband is the provider and the wife is the administrator of the house. Thus she is not expected to work. However, if she wishes to work some craft from home, she may do so, but what she earns from this falls in the same common household funds as the earnings of her husband and not her Strīdhana.

Kātyāyana Smṛti 904

“What she earns by working crafts, and what is given by strangers, there the husband has authority also. The rest is Strīdhana.”

This is in the case that the woman’s husband is providing for her. If he goes away without making monetary preparations for her, she may work without question (Manusmṛti 9.75):

“When the husband has gone abroad without providing for her, she shall subsist by unobjectionable industries. ”

The Arthasastra of Kautilya also mentions that the state should employ the women without providers in such industries (2.23.2):

“And the king should have wool, tree-bark fibre, cotton, floss, hemp, linen (and so on) manufactured by widows, women with disabilities, unmarried girls, female ascetics, women, women who are serving a sentence, prostitutes, women supported only by their mothers, elderly royal servants, retired attendants and temple women”

Such examples are also in Itihasa, such as Draupadi who worked as a Sairandhri ( hairdresser) for the queen of Virata during her incognito exile, mentioned in book 4 of the Mahābhārata.

Inheritance

Of the man having sons

When a man dies, the first and foremost share is of his wife. Only after she has been given her share can there be any further partition:

Brhaspati smṛti 1.26.84

“ Similarly the widow’s share is enjoined for her clothing, food, etc. only after setting aside her share may further partition be done.”

Regarding the exact size of her share, the Yājñavalkya Smṛti (2.114) states:

“If the father does the partition amongst the sons, he may, as he wishes, give a special share to the elder son (Jyeṣṭhāṃśa) or give everyone an equal part (Samāṃśa). If he makes an equal partition, then the share of the wife must also be made equal.”

The father may will his property to be divided in one of two ways, Jyeṣṭhāṃśa and Samāṃśa. The former was a form where there was not really a partition: the elder son replaced the father, getting most of the property but also the responsibility of the mother and younger siblings. This form is, as briefly seen earlier, Kalivarjya. Hence only Samāṃśa may be done in Kaliyuga. In that system the wife’s share must be equal to that of the sons.

For the married daughters, as they receive Strīdhana from their family and others at marriage, and have their own share in the husband’s property, they receive no separate inheritance from the father upon death.

The case of unmarried daughters is different, however. As they have neither been given their due Strīdhana at marriage nor do they have a husband to maintain them and in whose property they have a share, they must receive inheritance upon their father’s death:

Manusmṛti 9.118

“From their respective shares, each brother must separately give a fourth part to the unmarried girls; refusing to do so, they shall become fallen.”

If this fourth-share from each brother is not enough for the required purpose of marriage and maintenance, then they shall receive the same share as a brother.

Kātyāyana Smṛti 858

“Of the unmarried girls, a fourth part is ordained; each son shall get only three parts of his share. If the share is insufficient, all shall receive equal shares.”

Finally, an illustration: let us say a man died leaving his widow, two sons, and two daughters, one of whom is unmarried. In the first place, the widow shall get her equal share with the two sons, i.e., 33.3%, and only then will further partition be done. After that, for the unmarried girl, each brother shall give a fourth part from his 33.3% share, i.e., 8.3% or around 16.6% in total for the girl, both brothers receiving 25%. If that is not sufficient for the marriage and maintenance of the girl, each sibling shall receive an equal share of 22.2% each. In any case, no portion is set aside for the married girl.

Inheritance of the man having only daughters

If a man has only daughters and no sons, he may declare his daughter to be an “appointed daughter” (Putrikā):

Manusmṛti 9.127–128

“He who has no son may make his daughter an ‘appointed daughter’ in the following manner: [He shall make this declaration:] ‘The child that may be born of her shall be the performer of my funeral rites.’”

In ancient times Dakṣa Prajāpati himself made appointed daughters in this same manner, for the purpose of multiplying his race.

Note that declaring the daughter to be a Putrikā is a mere formality; in actuality, the daughters of a sonless man are Putrikās regardless of any declaration. The Brahma Purāṇa, as quoted in the Vivādaratanākara of Ācārya Caṇḍeśvara (1589), states:

“A man without a son who declared his daughter to be like a son, even mentally, or before the king, the sacred fire, relatives, or anywhere else, or before her pregnancy, or when she is given to a suitor, or even if she is given in marriage after her father has died, should be known as a Putrikā. From the paternal inheritance she receives an equal share as a son.”

Thus, wherever the father declares her to be a Putrikā, or even if he does not do so and merely mentally considers her so, in all cases she is a Putrikā. She continues both the spiritual lineage, by her son performing the funeral and ancestral rites, and the legal lineage, by inheriting the property just as a son would, though married.

“Women are inviolable, thus state the knowers of dharma as the conclusive dharma.”

“Women are created inviolable, thus accept the thinkers of dharma.”

“No being should strike women, even if they are at fault.”

“Women are inviolable, even those amongst the animal species.”

Domestic violence

Another frequently misinterpreted verse is Manusmiti 8.299:

“ The wife, the son, the servant and the younger brother, if they have committed fault, are fit to be beaten (Tadana).”

In the case of the son, younger brother and servant, one may take the standard meaning of “Tadana” , i.e. physically beating, as this is corroborated by other dharamsastras as well (Yajfiavalkya 1.155

“qa farsi a arsae ) in the case of the wife however, such a meaning cannot be taken, as that would contradict the dozens of pramanas, including from the sruti, that women should never be struck, even if they are at fault.

Accordingly, Medhatithi states:

“Here merely guiding them to the right path is ordained, and not actually beating. By this it should be understood that verbal ‘beating’ should be done.”

Thus the word “Tadana” in this verse must be taken to mean verbal censure on when any of the listed members have committed a fault, and not actual physical beating.

“Whatever is the fine or punishment for a man for any crime, it shall be half in the case of women. If the punishment for the man is death, for women it shall be cutting off a limb.”

Garments

The principal injunction regarding clothing for women comes from the Śaṅkha-likhita Dharmasūtra, as quoted in Mitākṣarā, Viramitrodaya, Smṛtimuktāvalī, and many other texts. It states:

“She should not show her navel. She should drape the clothing till her ankles. Her breasts should not be open. She should not go out without her Uttarīya.”

On purdah

e On “purdah”

A very commonly accepted idea amongst Hindus, especially in the north and especially in the rural areas, is that women ought to cover their head, or even their face. One may see women of the house in the north rush to drape the ghunghat over their hair and face when a man other than their husband or blood relatives enters the home, or put their Saree’s dupatta over their hair when entering a temple. Even in the shows or films adapting stories from Hindu scriptures, all the good women cover their head, and only the demonic women are shown without such covering.

One may notice, however, that the śāstras enjoin women to cover their lower body from the navel down to slightly above the ankles, and their chest, but nothing at all is stated about covering the head or face. In fact, the numerous head and face ornaments shown just above, would all become invisible and thereby useless, if the head or face were covered.

If this practice is not in the scriptures, then where has it come from? Both the word ‘Purdah’, which is a Persian word, and its geographic prevalence, the north of India, which was occupied by Muslim powers for far longer than the south, give us the answer. It is a borrowing of a muslim concept, and not a Hindu Scriptural one. Historian A.S. Altekar writes in his book, ‘the position of women in hindu

“There are absolutely no traces of any Purdah observed within the family in the Hindu period. The general adoption of the Purdah system by the ruling and aristocratic families of Hindu community is subsequent to the advent of the Muslim rule. It was accepted by Hindu society in imitation of the manners of the conquerors.”

Further he states:

“This happened almost universally in northern India, where the Muslim rule and culture were in ascendancy for a long time. In the Deccan, the Muslim influence was superficial, and so the Purdah system got no footing in the Hindu society there ”

Not only is the practice itself absent from Hindu scriptures and pre-islamic Hindu society as a whole, but even the rationale for it is explicitly rejected. In Islam, purdah is done to protect the women of the house from the male gaze. However, lord Rama himself states :

IV. Purity

Sāmānya dharmas

Dharma, in general, is highly subject to the individual. It is different depending on one's Varṇa, Āśrama and gender, as we have seen the special injunctions for women on many issues. A few dharmas, however, are common (Samanya) to all.

The Pulastya Smṛti states (22):

(Śauca), compassion and patience; these are common dharmas for people of every Varṇa and gender”

Most of these are self-explanatory. Only Ahimsa and Śauca require some elaboration.

Ahimsa: In the case of women the primary application of Ahimsa is with regards to diet. The Mahābhārata states (13.116.25-26)

“Ahimsa is the greatest dharma , ahimsa is the greatest tapas. Ahimsa is the greatest truth, from which dharma proceeds. Meat is not procured from grasses, trees or rocks, only by killing a being is there meat. Therefore there is fault in eating it.”

However the exceptions to this are also stated. (Manusmṛti 5.27)

“ One may eat meat which is sacrificed with proper procedure, or for feeding brāhmaṇas, or upon threat to his life.”

Regular purification (Nitya śauca)

Purification can be divided on two bases. First, that which is to be done daily (Nitya), and second, that which is to be done due to impurity from a particular occasion (Naimittika). Of these, we shall first see Nitya.

Further, Purity is divided as internal and external (Daksa smṛti 5.6):

“Purity is of two types, viz., external and internal. Better than complete impurity is external purity; better than external purity is internal purity.”

Thus, internal impurity is the most important. A very famous verse of the Brahmavaivarta purāṇa (1.17.17) states:

“Whether he is externally impure or pure, or whatever state he is in, the one who thinks of the supreme lord, is pure from inside and out”

For this reason, both husband and wife are enjoined to focus their minds on the supreme lord—Viṣṇu or Śiva or Devī, depending on sect—immediately after waking up (Vyāsa Smṛti, as quoted in Strīdharmapaddhati and Smṛtimuktāphala):

“ The night is divided into 6 sections (called Muhirtas), one should wake up in the final one (Brahma muhtrta, around an hour before sunrise) and, along with his wife, focus his mind on the supreme lord. Only thereafter he should think of matters of ritual and business.”

Occasional purification (Naimittika śauca)

The method of external purity is bathing, as stated in Dakṣa Smṛti 2.6–7:

“With the approach of dawn one should perform Śauca (here, urination and defecation) as per the procedure. Thereafter, one should brush his teeth and bathe. The body, having nine openings, becomes extremely impure due to the secretions from these (sweat, etc.) day and night. By the morning bath, it is purified.”

Menstruation

Earlier it was seen that, due to the gift of three gods, women are considered always pure. Yet now menstruation, a natural part of womanhood itself, is a state of impurity requiring purification? Is this not contradictory? No; in fact, one is the direct consequence of the other. The Baudhāyana Dharmasūtra states (2.2.4.3–5):

“Women have an unparalleled means of purification, because of which nothing renders them permanently impure. Every month, their menstrual blood removes all their impurity.”

“Soma gave them external purity, Gāndharva gave them pure speech, and Agni gave them ritual purity. Therefore women have no impurity.”

Similarly Manusmiti (5.107-108)

“What needs purification is purified by clay and water; the river is purified by its current; A woman, if impure even by mind, by menstruation; and Brāhmaṇas by renunciation.”

Therefore the women, whose menstruation is compared to a flowing river, are purer than men who are thereby like still water.

Thus menstruation is framed not as a defect of womanhood, but a great merit. It is not an impurity, rather it is supreme purity that manifests through temporary impurity.

Certain rules also have to be followed during this time, such as avoiding sex (Manusmiti 4.40):

“one should never (sexually) approach a menstruating woman, even if he is mad with desire.”

It was earlier seen that the husband is enjoined to take care of the wife during menstruation by the Kātyāyana smṛti. This is because at this time they are told to cease religious and household duties:

the Angiras smṛti states (37) :

“She should not perform her religious duties (Sadhvacara) until there is flow of menstrual blood. When it has stopped, she may resume household work and other activities, having bathed on the fourth day (after the start of menstruation), she is purified.”

Childbirth

Many cultures observe a designated period of impurity of the mother after childbirth. The Hindu scriptures are unique, however, in that the impurity (called Sitaka) applies to the father as well.

The Baudhayana dharmasttra states (1.5.11

19-23):

“ The impurity for both childbirth and death in the family is the same, i.e., ten nights. In the case of childbirth there is a ten day impurity for both mother and father. Some say it is only for the mother, as she is the one who has given birth. Some others state it is the father, because of the contribution of semen. The correct view is that it is for both, due to their mutual contribution.”

V. Education

Education in the Vedas

The discussion of women’s education in the Hindu scriptures must be separated into two discussions: (i) education in the Vedas, and (ii) education in all topics other than the Vedas, for these are very different.

The general restriction of women from Vedic study

The Vedas are the most sacred scriptures of Hinduism. They are not a book that is bought and read; rather, they are only heard (hence also called Śruti, “that which is heard”) from a guru. This hearing is not simple either: it begins with initiation through the sacred thread ceremony, which is performed only for the first three Varṇas and not for the Śūdra. After this the child goes to live with the guru and study under him, serving him and following hundreds, if not thousands, of rules. During this time he must beg for alms (Bhikṣāṭana). Only then may he learn the Vedic mantras and their use in yajñas (fire rituals) from the guru.

In the case of general women, however, the Manusmṛti (2.67) states:

“For women, marriage is equivalent to the Vedic initiation rite, serving the husband is equivalent to living with and serving the guru, and the performance of household duties is equivalent to the performance of Vedic fire rituals.”

Therefore women in general are not enjoined to study the Vedas. It is for them (and the Śūdras and uninitiated) that the other Vedic literatures—the Itihāsas and Purāṇas, called the fifth Veda—have been composed.

Regarding this the Bhagavata purāṇa (1.4.25) states:

“ for the Women, Śūdras and uninitiated men varṇas there is not study of the Sruti. for their benefit, out of compassion the sage Vyasa composed the Mahābhārata.”

Similarly Devi bhagavata purāṇa 1.3.21

“ For women, Śūdras and uninitiates amongst the other three varṇas, study of the Vedas is not accepted. Is for their benefit that the Purāṇas were created.”

The exception: Brahmavādinīs and Uttamastrīs

As emphasised however, this restriction is a general rule and is subject to any exceptions that may be enjoined regarding it in the scriptures. And exceptions there must be, because we see in the scriptures that several hymns of the Vedas have themselves been composed by women, and in the Upaniṣads, Itihasas and purāṇas all mention women learned in Vedas, such as Gargi, Sulabha and Madālasā. Thus, the higher (Uttama) category of women, called Brahmavādinīs, have been enjoined to study Vedas, as an exception to the general rule. The principle verse on this is Harita smṛti 21.23:

“Women are of two types, Brahmavādinīs and sadyovadhis. Amongst them, of the Brahmavādinīs, the Vedic initiation, fire rituals, study of the Vedas and Bhiksatana within their own house is enjoined. Of the Sadhyovadhis, marriage (is the equivalent)”.

Thus all the earlier verses seen pertain to the Sadyovadhūs, and not to the Brahmavādinīs.

Amongst Acaryas, Madhvācārya, the founder of one of the three greatest schools of Vedānta and one of the most influential Acaryas of all time, has repeatedly mentioned the exception of Brahmavādinīs in his writings. He writes in his Brahma sūtra bhasya ,1.1.1:

“ Of the Women, Śūdras and uninitiates of the other three Varṇas, there is right to the study of Tantra ( as opposed to Veda). Of the Higher category of women, however, right to Vedas is also stated.”

And in his Mahābhārata-tatparya-nirnaya, 29.33

“ Everything should be studied by the women of the three Varṇas, except the Vedas. By the Higher category of women, such as Draupadi, even the Vedas are to be studied. This “Higher category” includes Goddesses, wives of the sages, and even those born in Human births.”

This should be noted for the later discussion of study of topics other than Vedas as well. He also writes in the Apasudradhikarana (1.3.36) of the Brahma sitras, the section which restricts Vedas for Śūdras:

“ The case of the higher category of women is not the same as the Śūdras, because by mantras such as “May my co-wife be destroyed” (Ṛgveda saṃhitā 10.145.2) their right to the Vedas must be admitted.”

“If it is objected that they do not have the initiatory ritual, then that is only the general rule. There is also initiation for them similar to the Upanayana enjoined in the Smṛti (Harita).”

Madhva argues from a Ṛgveda mantra that states “May my co-wife be destroyed.” This mantra can only be used by someone who has co-wives in the first place, that is, a woman. Therefore at least some women must be admitted to have the right to the Vedas; otherwise this mantra would have no application (Niravakāśa), which cannot be true according to Mīmāṃsā. This is a more forceful argument than was discussed earlier.

Further, he states that the objection that the initiatory ritual (Saṃskāra) is not enjoined for women (due to the verse of Manusmṛti) also concerns only the general rule. The exceptional initiation of Brahmavādinīs is stated in the Smṛti (that is, Harita, though Madhva does not mention it by name). Jayatīrtha, the foremost sub-commentator on Madhva, states this in plainer terms:

“The sense is that the lack of right to Vedic study is merely a general rule, and not an absolute one. Therefore, by a more specific exception there is no issue.”

Thus the exception of Brahmavādinī/Uttamastrī to the general restriction is well established by both scriptures and Ācāryas.

Yama smṛti states —‘In the earlier age (purakalpe) , the tying of sacred thread is expected for women, as well as teaching them the Vedas and chanting Gayatri mantra. Firstly, the meaning of the term “Purakalpe” being “ in an earlier age” is itself disputable, but let us discuss that later.

Assuming the meaning to be “In an earlier age”, this argument is similar to the one made earlier by Vidyaranya, regarding remarriage being forbidden in this yuga. That argument was decidedly stronger than this one, however, for that one had some injunctive force (“these are forbidden in Kali”) whereas this one has none.

All that can be inferred from something being practiced in an earlier age is that it has lost prevalence now, it does not necessitate an outright prohibition on it. Though this is obvious in and of itself, it may be demonstrated with the following example from Mahābhārata (13.116.53) :

“Tt is heard that in an earlier age (Purakalpe) amongst men that animals would be made out of grain, and officiaters, focused on attaining the higher realms, would perform sacrifice with them”

This practice is corroborated in the Manusmiti (5.37) :

“Tf there is occasion for sacrifice, one may make animals out of ghee or grains (for the sacrifice). Never should one wish to harm an animal

This practice is recognised, even by those from the lineages of Devana or Vidyāraṇya, to be a valid practice even this age. No one objects to it on the basis of “Purākalpe”. Even they recognise, here at least, that it has no injunctive force.

Let us see another verse from the Mahābhārata (6.41.18)

“It is heard in an earlier age (Purākalpe) that one who goes to war without permission of his preceptors is cursed with evil thoughts by those great ones.”

The meaning of “in an earlier age” simply does not fit here. That is why the gloss given by Nīlakaṇṭha in his commentary, Bhāratabhāvādīpa, is “Purākalpe means ‘in the ancient scriptures [Vedas].’”

Indeed, a careful reading of the Yama smṛti would reveal that this alternate meaning is the one that fits in that verse as well. Because each and every verb in that verse is present tense and injunctive (isyate, adhyapayet, vidhtyate). None is past tense. It is clearly making an injunction in the current age, not stating one that was once practiced in a bygone era and is now forbidden.

Even if the Purvapaksa can argue that the earlier age meaning is superior, however, it does not matter. Because, even overlooking the lack of injunctive force in their meaning, there would be ambiguity. And an ambiguous passage can never be used to restrict an unambiguous one, such as that of Harita.

VI. Religion

Religious activities

In truth, several forms of religious activity have already been discussed. The household and marital duties are the highest religious duties of the women, even by their performance alone she can attain the highest good, all else is optional. Besides them we discussed purity, both external as well as internal, the latter consisting of meditation on God. The study of scriptures (Svadhayaya) is also a form of religious activity. Now let us see all the ones that remain, viz, (1) the obligatory occasional rituals (Samskaras), (II) Fasting (Vrata), (II) pilgrimage (Tīrtha), (IV) chanting, (V) making offerings to the gods and (VI) charity (Dana).

Saṃskāras

There are a total of 16 obligatory occasional (Naimittika) rituals in Hindu scriptures. Of these, all 16 are performed for men of the three higher Varṇas (and Brahmavādinīs) using Vedic mantras. For general women, however, only the ten are formed, and without vedic mantras. The ones excluded are those that relate to study of the Vedas (Sacred thread, first alms, graduation, etc) for which marriage has been stated to be the equivalent. The Vyasa smṛti states :

“The samskaras are reckoned to be sixteen. Of these, nine, ending with ‘Karnavedhana’ are to be performed for women, without mantras. Marriage is performed for them with mantras, except the Śūdras for whom it is without, thus totalling ten.”

It then lists there names and when they are to be done:

  1. Garbhādhāna: the rite to mark conception in the first month.
  2. Puṃsavana: prayers for the fetus in the third month.
  3. Sīmantā: ceremony of parting the mother’s hair in the eighth month.
  4. Jātakarma: the birth rites.
  5. Nāmakaraṇa: naming ceremony on the eleventh day.
  6. Niṣkramaṇa: first outing in the fourth month.
  7. Annaprāśana: first feeding in the sixth month.
  8. Cūḍākarmā: first haircut, as per family tradition.
  9. Karṇavedhana: ear-piercing after the Cūḍākarmā.

Fasting (Vrata)

For all the topics henceforth there is pretty much no difference by gender. Any injunction for men is also valid for women, unless explicitly stated otherwise, because there is no restriction on women stated for these. However, for demonstration, we will see only those verses which explicitly enjoin the activity for women.

“Listen, O Nārada, I shall narrate that fasts of the 3rd night of the fortnight, which a woman, by doing properly, attains great fortune.”

Linga purāṇa 84.5

“ One should perform fast for Śiva, the supreme. Doing so, he attains oneness with Śiva (if a man), or with Devi, if a woman, O lord.”

“The one who bathes at that holy site of Viṣṇu on the third day of the month of Māgha and worships Viṣṇu with devotion, whether man or woman, is freed of all sins.”

on pilgrimage to prabhasa ksetra:

Mokṣa

Examples of Brahmavādinīs such as Maitreyī and Sulabhā, who received education in Mokṣa-śāstra, have already been seen. Even so, some verses that directly state women’s eligibility will now be presented.

The most famous verse on the topic is perhaps Bhagavad Gītā (9.32):

“Whoever surrenders unto me, O son of Prtha! Be they those of sinful birth, Women, vaiśyas or Śūdras, they also attain the supreme destination. ”

Thus the Mokṣa of women is stated plainly in the Gītā itself.

On the translation of Gītā 9.32

This verse is, however, less famous as one which uplifts women and lower varṇas by acknowledging their eligibility for Mokṣa, and more famous for the alleged accusation that it calls Women and Śūdras as ‘those of sinful birth’ (Papayoni). The majority of translations, including Gītā press, the most widely read translation in India, as well as Adidevananda, Shivananda, Tejomayananda, etc have translated it correctly ( Papayoni as a separate category). However, ISKCON’s ‘As it is’, the most widely read translation outside India, has mistranslated it, and a few others like A.M Shastri, by misuse of punctuation:

ISKCON

O son of Prtha, those who take shelter in Me, though they be of lower birth - women, vaiśyas [merchants] and śūdras [workers] - can attain the supreme destination.

A.M. Shastri

For, finding refuge in Me, they also who, O son of Pritha, may be of a sinful birth—women, vaiśyas as well as śūdras—even they attain to the Supreme Goal.

This reading is invalid grammatically, by the commentarial tradition, and by scripture. Grammatically, there is nothing in the verse to indicate that ‘Papayoni’ is the category and the other three terms mentioned come under it, It is stated as one continous list. By the commentarial tradition, Acarya Rāmānuja, founder of the Viśiṣṭādvaita tradition, clarifies :

“ Women, Vaiśyas, Śūdras and also (Ca api) the papayonis, surrendering unto me, attain the supreme destination.”

Acarya Śrīdhara of the Advaita tradition has also explained each term

“ Be they Papayonis, I.e those of sinful birth like the mixed castes, or be they Vaiśyas ,i.e ones focused on agriculture and such activities, or be they Women and Śūdras, who are devoid of study of the Vedas, all of them, surrendering to me, serving me, attain the supreme destination. This is certain”

As per Śrīdhara, ‘Papayoni’ refers to the mixed castes, Because intermarriage between two Varṇas itself is sinful, and so those birthed by it are ‘of sinful birth’

Similarly Vallabha:

“Women (such as) the ladies of Vraja, who had only love (Bhāva) for Kṛṣṇa, Vaiśyas such as those mentioned in the Mahābhārata like Nanda or other inhabitants of Vraja, and famous Śūdras like Vidura, and others like the Pāpayonis, i.e. those born in lower races like the Huns, Greeks, Pulindas, etc., by taking refuge in Me, the Supreme Being, the ocean of parental love, the ocean of compassion, the purifier of the most fallen, the refuge of the one who has no other refuge, the Lord of Vraja, by whatever emotion, merely by that act of taking refuge attain the supreme destination.”

Vallabha interprets “Pāpayoni” to be the Mleccha races, such as Huns and Greeks. This is supported by the Bhāgavatam:

“Salutations to Viṣṇu, who purifies even the Kirāta, Hūṇa, Āndhra, Pulinda, Pulkasa, Ābhīra, Śumbha, Greek, Khasa, and other such sinful (Pāpa) races, who are called so because of their addiction to sinful activities.”

The Purāṇas also clearly mention “Pāpayoni” as a separate category from Śūdras and Vaiśyas while enumerating all the categories of people (Nārada Purāṇa 1.48.51 and Matsya Purāṇa 180.19):

“Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, Śūdras, and the mixtures of these Varṇas, also the demonic Mlecchas, and the mixed Pāpayonis.”

Here ‘Papayoni’ has been indicated to be the mixture of People of the four Varṇas and Mlechhas.

Thus, by grammar, by the commentarial tradition, and most importantly by the scripture itself, any notion of women being ‘Papayonis’ has been dispelled.

Women for Mokṣa

Finally, let us conclude with some more verses stating the eligibility of women for Mokṣa:

Padma Purāṇa 6.30.116

“Those who are steadfastly engaged in Bhakti, be they men or women, are all freed from sins and attain the supreme Brahman.”

Brahma Purāṇa 227.13–14

“Blessed are those who always worship Lord Viṣṇu, the God who destroys all sins and fulfils all desires, be they Brāhmaṇas, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, Śūdras, or mixed castes. By worshipping him, the best of gods, they attain the supreme destination.”

Skanda Purāṇa 3.3.5.10

“The mortal who is endowed with devotion to Lord Śiva, be they even a Pulkaśa or Caṇḍāla, whether man, woman, or intersex, is immediately liberated from this material world.”

Matsya Purāṇa 60.12 and Padma Purāṇa 1.29.13

“By worshipping Devī with devotion, who is of the nature of good fortune and giver of material benefits and liberation, what does one, be it a man or woman, not attain?”

4. Addendum

I. Conclusion

Thus, almost every topic of a woman’s life—legal, social, and religious—has been explained, with scripture as the primary divine guide, without historicisation, and with the Ācāryas as supporting evidence. All the topics discussed may now be summarised by chronology in the life of a woman.

When she is born, one ought to rejoice, for Devī herself has come to his home, and the daughter is equal or superior to the son. Her saṃskāras should be performed, and she should be given education. If she is exceptional in religious topics, her sacred thread initiation should be done and she taught the Vedas as well, though not otherwise. When she reaches the appropriate age, she should be married in one of the higher forms of marriage, without exchange of money and with her consent, to a suitable husband. If a suitable husband is not found, she may remain unmarried, though marriage is superior as a way to pay the ṛṇas and enter the Gṛhastha stage.

When she is married, she should be loved, served, provided for, and protected by her husband, for whom she is the supreme refuge. She too should serve him, perform household work such as cooking and cleaning, and advise him on matters of family and business. When she has children, she is more venerable to them than their father or teacher. If her husband has gone away for a long time, is impotent, has taken renunciation, or has fallen, then she may leave him and remarry. If he dies, she may live as a widow or remarry; she should not perform Sahagamana.

Whatever money she receives is her own and may not be taken by anyone, or else they will be legally punished. If her husband has gone away without making arrangements for her, she may work in respectable industries.

She receives inheritance from her father if there are no sons, or if she is unmarried. She receives inheritance from her husband, equal to a son, or the entirety of it if there are no children. Upon her own death, her property is inherited by her daughters, by her sons if there are no daughters, or by her husband or parents, depending on the type of marriage.

She is to be guarded—not dominated—by her father in childhood, her husband in youth, and her sons in old age. If not, by the government or king. She is inviolable. Any verbal, physical, or sexual offence against her is a more serious crime than against a man. Abandonment and adultery are punishable offences.

She should wear clothing that covers her chest and lower body from navel to ankle. If she goes out, she should take an Uttarīya over her arms and/or shoulder. All else is optional. She should wear the marital ornaments always, and other ornaments as she wishes. There is no fault in her going out in public.

Menstruation is the unparalleled means of purification for women, like the flow of a river. Though it leaves them temporarily impure during that time, they should cease religious and household activities then. At childbirth, both she and her husband are impure for ten days. She should maintain internal purity by meditating on God with her husband, and external purity by bathing.

She may perform religious activities like fasting, pilgrimage, chanting, making offerings, and charity, though fulfilling her household duties itself is also sufficient. By performing bhakti of her preferred deity, she attains Mokṣa.

Some may call the scriptural views “progressive,” though this is a very bad label, because progress implies gradual improvement from a less than ideal state. The scriptures, however, are eternal. The only issue is misinterpretation and addition. Thus what is to be done is not “progress” but “purification” to arrive at the original scriptural view.
