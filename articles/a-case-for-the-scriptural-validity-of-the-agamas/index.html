1 Introduction

1.1 Preface & Core Objective

The Problem: While the Vedas are universally honored as Śruti (the highest revealed knowledge), the vast corpus of Āgamas has frequently been dismissed or demeaned as non-Vedic (avaidika), delusory, or deceitful due to misinterpretation and isolated proof-texting.

Purpose: To systematically establish the objective scriptural position of the Āgamas and demonstrate their inherent validity (Prāmān.ya) across all traditional Hindu streams.

Primary Methodology: Rooted in the primary authority of Śruti (Veda-Sahitās, Brāhman.as, Āran.yakas, and Upanis.ads) and extended through Itihāsa, Purān.a, and Smr.ti. The framework subordinates post-scriptural human opinion to explicit scriptural injunctions and applies Mı̄māsā hermeneutics (Samanvaya) to reconcile apparent contradictions rather than discarding texts.

2 Scriptural Extension of Authority & The Scope of Revelation

2.1 Expansion of Valid Authority Beyond the Four Vedas

First, let us examine the claim that the only valid scripture for Hindus is the Vedas. This premise is proven incorrect by the Vedas themselves.

Extension to Smr.ti

In the Taittirı̄ya Sahitā of the Yajurveda (2.2.10.2), Manusmr.ti is explicitly referenced:

yad vai manuravadat tad bhes.ajam

“Whatever Manu said is medicine.” Because the authority and validity of the Manusmr.ti is established directly by the Veda, anyone rejecting the Manusmr.ti must simultaneously reject the validity of the Veda itself. This is how the category of Śāstra (scripture) is extended to authoritative texts outside of Śruti.

Extension to Itihāsa and Purān.a

In the Chāndogya Upanis.ad (7.1.4), it is stated: nāma vā r.gvedo yajurvedah. sāmaveda ātharvan.aścaturtha itihāsapurān.ah. pañcamo vedānām . vedah. pitryo rāśirdaivo nidhirvākovākyamekāyanam . devavidyā brahmavidyā bhūtavidyā ks.atravidyā naks.atravidyā sarpadevajanavidyā nāmaivaitannāmopāssveti —— 7.1.4 ——

“Name indeed is the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda, the Atharvaveda as the fourth, the Itihasa-Purana as the fifth among the Vedas...” Therefore, the category of the Veda—and Śruti-level authority by extension—includes the Itihāsas (such as the Rāmāyan.a, Mahābhārata, and Śiva Rahasya Itihāsa) as well as the 36 Purān.as codified by Vedavyāsa (consisting of the 18 Mahā-Purān.as and 18 Upā-Purān.as).

Validation of Smr.tis through Itihāsa and Purān.a The validity of the Smr.tis is further established across multiple Purān.as and Itihāsas. For instance, the Mahābhārata (Book 14, Aśvamedhika Parva, Chapter 96) enumerates an extensive lineage of authoritative Smr.tis:

  • Manu, Vasis.t.ha, Kaśyapa, Gārgya, Gautama, and Gopālaka: Codes of law authored by these ancient sages.
  • Parāśara, Maitreya, Umā, Maheśvara, and Nandi: Ancient teachings, including the holy doctrines of Umā and Maheśvara and the purifying laws of Nandi.
  • Brahmā, Sanatkumāra (Kaumāra), Dhūmāyana, and Kān.d.a-Vaiśvānara: Divine laws and teachings spoken by Brahmā and other exalted figures.
  • Bhārgava, Yājñavalkya, Mārkan.d.eya, Bharadvāja, and Br.haspati: Authoritative law codes composed by these prominent rishis.
  • Kun.i, Kun.ibāhu, Viśvāmitra, Sumantu, Jaimini, and Śākuneya: Dharmic codes authored across various sage traditions.
  • Pulastya, Pulaha, Agni (Pāvakı̄ya), Agastya, Mudgala, Śān.d.ilya, and Śalābhayana: Sacred laws sung by primary sages and divine figures.
  • Vālakhilyas, Saptars.is (Seven Sages), Āpastamba, Śaṅkha, and Likhita: Essential legal and moral frameworks codified by these revered groups and seers.
  • Prajāpati, Yama, Mahendra, Vyāghra, Vyāsa, and Vibhan.d.aka: Traditions handed down through divine and patriarchal lineages.
  • Nārada, Kapota, Vidura, Bhrigu, and Aṅgiras: Wise sayings, dharmic traditions, and foundational moral codes.
  • Krauñca, Mr.daṅga, Sūrya (Solar Traditions), Hārı̄ta, Piśaṅga, Kat.apāya, and Suvālaka: Ancient sung traditions and specialized legal lineages.
  • Uddālaka, Uśanas (Śukrācārya), and Vaiśampāyana: Laws and teachings passed down through major classical lineages.

Furthermore, the Parāśara Smr.ti—one of the Smr.tis explicitly mentioned in the Mahābhārata—enumerates additional canonical texts in verses 1.1.12–1.1.15:

“O Father, please tell me about Dharma (righteousness/duty), for I deserve your grace. I have already heard of the codes of law (dharmas) authored by Manu, Vasistha, and Kashyapa;”

“...as well as those remembered from Gargya, Gautama, Ushanas (Shukracharya), Atri, Vishnu, Samvarta, Daksha, and Angiras;”

“...and those proclaimed by Shatatapa, Harita, Yajnavalkya, Apastamba, Shankha, and Likhita;”

“...as well as those composed by Katyayana and the sage Prachetas. I have heard all these as taught by you, and the teachings of the Vedic texts have not been forgotten by me.”

Because several of these Smr.tis cross-reference even more text lineages, this canonical list is illustrative rather than exhaustive.

In the Annapūrn.ā Upanis.ad (Chapter 3, Verse 21)—which is Śruti, belongs to the Atharvaveda, and is part of the Muktikā Canon of 108 Upanis.ads—the Śaiva Āgamas are not subordinated, but are explicitly identified as the ultimate conclusion of all Śāstras, including the Vedas themselves:

śivah. śaivāgamasthānā kālah. kālaikavādinām — yatsarvaśāstrasiddhānta yatsarvahr.dayānugamam —— 21 —— “What Shiva is to the followers of the Shaiva Agamas, and as Time to those who uphold Time as the ultimate truth. It is the ultimate conclusion of all scriptures and that which resonates within every heart.”

Furthermore, the Chāndogya Upanis.ad (7.1.2) references the Āgamas under another traditional designation, Ekayāna:

r.gvedam . bhagavo’dhyemi yajurvedam . sāmavedamātharvan.am . caturthamitihāsapurān.am . pañcamam. vedānām . vedam. pitryam. rāśim . daivam . nidhim . vākovākyamekāyanam . devavidyām . brahmavidyām. bhūtavidyām. ks.atravidyām . naks.atravidyām . sarpadevajanavidyāmetadbhagavo’dhyemi —— 7.1.2 ——

In his work Āgama Prāmān.ya, Yāmunācārya explicitly identifies Ekayāna as referring to the Kashmir-Āgamas, affirming them as apaurus.eya (authorless/eternal) and treating them as Śruti when examining this verse:

ekāyanaśākhāyā apaurus.eyatva tathā kāśmı̄rāgamaprāmān.ye prapañcitamiti neha prastūyate — “...the eternality/authoritativeness of the Ekayana Shakha has been detailed by me in [the work] Kashmira-Agama-Pramanya, so it is not discussed here.”

Pūrva Paks.a Objection: The Ekayāna Śākhā only refers to Vais.n.ava or Pāñcarātra works, not other Āgamas.

Refutation & Validation: The Āgamas are explicitly acknowledged and validated by Vedavyāsa himself in the Mahābhārata (Śānti Parva, Moks.adharma Parva, Chapter 349, Verse 64). Vyāsa asserts that speculative reasoning (hetu) cannot be used to invalidate either the Pāśupata or Pāñcarātra texts along with the Vedas, as they possess intrinsic validity (ātmapramān.a): sāṅkhyam. yogah. pāñcarātram . vedāh. pāśupatam . tathā — ātmapramān.ānyetāni na hantavyāni hetubhih. —— “Sankhya, Yoga, Pancaratra, the Vedas, and the Pashupata—these are self-evident authorities (atmapramana) and should not be invalidated by logical arguments.” Furthermore, prominent Ācāryas within Yāmunācārya’s own Śrı̄ Vais.n.ava lineage confirm that the scope of authoritative Āgamas extends beyond Vais.n.ava texts. Vedānta Deśika, in his Pāñcarātra Raks.ā, explicitly accepts the validity of the Pāśupata Āgamas while commenting on the aforementioned Mahābhārata verse: ata eva bhagavatā-pi śivādi-rūpāntarair-yathārhādhikāri-phala-viśes.ārthibhyastadupadeśāh. kr.tāh. — “Accordingly, the Lord (as Siva) revealed the Sastra for the sake of those who sought particular fruits (phala-viśes.ārthibhyah.) and in accordance with their respective fitness.” tataśca sāṅkhyādı̄nām . tatra tatra pr.thagupadis..ta-phala-viśes.a-prāptiparatayā prāmān.yam-iti siddham — “...it follows, therefore, that the Sankhya and other systems are valid for those who seek the particular results (phala-viśes.āh.) mentioned in them.” To reinforce this point, Vedānta Deśika cites Lord Śiva directly from the Ahirbudhnya Samhitā (12.4–5): sāṅkhyam . yoga-statha-iv-anya-ch-chāstram. yat-paramam . tapah. — vistaren.a hi tasyoktam . mayā-iva vidureti vai ——

“Sankhya, Yoga, and other systems which are supreme austerity — these were spoken at length by Me alone, O Vidura.” Thus, non-Vais.n.ava Āgamas are explicitly acknowledged by Śrı̄ Vais.n.ava Ācāryas as part of the broader Āgamic tradition identified with the Ekayāna Śākhā.

In the Śiva Purān.a (Kailāsa Sahitā, Chapter 21, Verses 9–10), the Śaiva Āgamas are explicitly prescribed by Lord Śiva through a Vidhi-Vākya (injunction) for aspirants seeking spiritual realization: adhairyyacitto yah. kaścitsamādhim . na ca vim . dati — tadupāyampravaks.yāmi sāvadhānatayā śr.n.u —— 9 —— “If there is such a person devoid of concentration who does not attain Samādhi, I shall mention the means of remedy for him. Listen attentively.” tripadārthaparijñānam . vedāntāgamavākyajam — śrutvā gurormukhādyogamabhyasetsa yamādikam —— 10 —— “On hearing from his preceptor the lore of Vedānta and Āgamas along with the explanation, he shall practise Yoga by means of Yama, Niyama, etc.”

Even if one attempts to dismiss references to the Śaiva Āgamas within the Śiva Purān.a under the pretext of sectarian categorization, the Padma Purān.a (Bhūmi-Khan.d.a, Chapter 69, Verse 1) independently designates the Śivadharma Āgamas as Uttama (supreme/excellent): atha dharmāh. śivenoktāh. śivadharmāgamottamāh. — jñeyā bahuvidhāste ca karmayogaprabhedatah. —— him. sādidos.anirmuktāh. kleśāyāsavivarjitāh. — sarvabhūtahitāh. śuddhāh. sūks.māyāsā mahatphalāh. —— 1 —— “Now, the supreme righteous duties (dharmas) spoken by Lord Shiva in the excellent Śivadharma Agamas are to be known in their many variations according to the divisions of Karma Yoga. They are free from faults such as violence (hisā), devoid of affliction and extreme exertion, beneficial to all living beings, pure, requiring subtle effort, yet yielding immense fruits.”

In the Skanda Purān.a (Sūta Samhitā, Yajña-vaibhava Khan.d.a, Chapter 22), the Bhairava, Śaiva, and Pāśupata Āgamas—along with the Vais.n.ava Āgamas (referring to the Pāñcarātra Āgamas) and even the Jain and Bauddha Agamas—are declared to possess validity (prāmān.ya) as principal Āgamas: athātah. sapravaks.yāmi mārgaprāmān.yanirn.ayam — śraddhayā sahitā yūya śr.n.udhva munisattamāh. ——

2.2 Recognition of the Āgamas across Itihāsa and Purāṇa

vedāśca dharmaśāstrān.i purān.a bhārata tathā — vedāṅgādyupavedāśca kāmikādyāgamānapi —— kāpāla nākala caiva tathopādāna dvijars.abhāh. — tathā pāśupata saumya bhairava—pramukhān gamān —— tes.āmevopabhedāśca śataśo’tha sahasraśah. — vis.n.uvāgamamāstathā bāhyān buddhārhadāgamānapi —— lokāyata tarkaśāstra bahuvistārasayutam — mı̄māsāmatigambhı̄rā sākhyayogau tathaiva ca —— ... tānyeva vistaren.aiva saṅgrahen.a vā punah. —— kurvanti tāni nāmāni kathitāni mahars.ibhih. — adhikārivibhedena naikarūpaiva sadā dvijāh. —— tarkairete hi mārgāstu na hantavyā manı̄s.ibhih. —— “Now I shall explain the decision regarding the authoritativeness of the paths (mārga). O best of sages, listen with faith! The Vedas, Dharma-shastras, Puranas, the Bharata (Mahabharata), the Vedangas, Upavedas, the Kamika and other Agamas, the Kapala, Nakula, Upadana, the Pashupata, Saumya, and Bhairava—these major Agamas, along with their sub-sects by the hundreds and thousands; the Vaishnava Agamas, and even the external Buddhist and Jaina Agamas, the Lokayata system, Logic (Tarka-shastra), the deep Mimamsa, and Sankhya and Yoga—the great sages have given names to these paths and taught them in detail or in brief according to the varying eligibility (adhikāra) of seekers. These paths should not be invalidated by wise men through logical arguments.” Specific emphasis is laid on the principle that each of these Āgamic systems is articulated according to the varying adhikāra (competence/qualification) of seekers. This framework of Adhikāra-Bheda is central to addressing passages in certain Purān.as that designate particular Āgamas as mohaka or delusory.

Pūrva Paks.a Objection: This verse from the Skanda Purān.a is merely Arthavāda (praise/exaggeration) and cannot be taken literally.

Refutation: The notion that statements within the Purān.as can be casually dismissed as Arthavāda is explicitly rejected in scripture. The Nārada Purān.a (Pūrva-Khan.d.a, Chapter 1, Verses 57–58) strictly prohibits reading Arthavāda into the Purān.as: purān.es.varthavādatva ye vadanti narādhamāh. —— 1-57 —— tairarjitāni pun.yāni ks.aya yānti dvijottamāh. — samastakarmanirmūlasādhanāni narādhamah. —— 1-58 —— purān.ānyarthavādena bruvannarakamaśnute —— “Those lowest among men who claim that the Purān.as contain Arthavāda—all their accumulated merit and virtue are

destroyed, O best of dvijas. By declaring the Purān.as to be Arthavāda, such wretched men go to hell.”

Pūrva Paks.a Objection: The Skanda Purān.a is classified as a Tāmasika Purān.a (e.g., in the Padma Purān.a) and its statements are therefore non-authoritative.

Refutation: This line of objection creates a mutual contradiction. While the Padma Purān.a classifies the Skanda Purān.a as Tāmasika, the Skanda Purān.a itself (Śaṅkara Sahitā, Daks.a Khan.d.a) conversely classifies the Vais.n.ava Purān.as—including the Padma Purān.a—as Tāmasika: vais.n.avāni ca catvāri tāmasāni manı̄śvarāh. — “O foremost of sages! The four Vaishnava Puranas are considered Tamasic. In them, the duties of the Kshatriyas (warrior class) are heard, and Hari (Vishnu) is their presiding deity.” Relying on one text to invalidate another when both make reciprocal claims results in Ātmāśraya (circular reasoning/self-dependence), which is an unacceptable defect in classical hermeneutics. According to Pūrva Mı̄māsā hermeneutics, scriptures must be interpreted through inherent authority rather than selective rejection. As stated in the Mı̄māsā Sūtras (1.1.5): autpattikastu śabdasyārthena sambandhah. — tasya jñānamupadeśah.; avyatirekaścārthe’napalabdhe — tatpramān.ambādarāyan.asyānapeks.atvāt —— 5 —— “On the other hand, the relation of the word with its meaning is inborn and eternal; consequently, injunction is the means of knowing dharma, and it is unfailing in regard to objects not perceived by other means of knowledge. It is authoritative, according to Bādarāyan.a, especially as it is independent and self-sufficient in its authority.” Therefore, discarding explicit scriptural statements by labeling them as ”absurd” or Tāmasika violates the foundational principle of Anapeks.atva (independent self-sufficiency of scriptural authority).

Pūrva Paks.a Objection: The Śaṅkara Samhitā and Sūta Samhitā are not legitimate parts of the Skanda Purān.a.

Context of the Objection: It is true that standard modern print editions (such as those from Gita Press) structure the Skanda Purān.a into seven Khan.d.as totaling 81,000 verses. This structure

is referenced in the Nārada Purān.a (Chapter 104, Verse 3), where Lord Brahmā states: skandāhvayastatra khan.d.āh. saptaiva parikalpitāh. — ekāśı̄tisahasram . tu skāndam . sarvoghakr.ntanam —— 3 —— “In that Purān.a named Skanda, seven sections (khan.d.as) have been designed. The Skanda Purān.a consists of eightyone thousand (ekāśı̄tisahasram) verses, and it destroys the entire accumulation of sins.”

Refutation & Textual Evidence: Lord Brahmā’s statement in the Nārada Purān.a enumerates a specific recension of 81,000 verses, but nowhere does it state that this represents the absolute limit or entirety of the Skanda Purān.a corpus. The internal testimony of the Skanda Purān.a’s own Samhitā tradition affirms a larger, expanded recension of 100,000 verses across six Sahitās: svatantramekamadvaitam . pravadanti niraṅkuśam — tatra skāndam . pravaks.yāmi purān.es.utrayodaśam —— 44 —— sahitābhih. tathā .sad.bhih. laks.airgranthairvirājitam — daśa caiva purān.āni sāttvikāni vidurbudhāh. —— 45 —— “They declare Him to be independent, one without a second (non-dual), and completely unrestricted. Among those Purān.as, I shall now narrate the Skanda Purān.a, which is the thirteenth. It shines brilliantly with six Samhitās and contains one hundred thousand verses. Wise scholars know that ten of the Purān.as are Sāttvika.” Combining the verse counts of the extant Samhitās (the Sūta Samhitā and Śaṅkara Samhitā) with the seven Khan.d.as yields precisely 100,000 verses, matching the internal verse count specified within the Samhitā text itself. Furthermore, this 100,000-verse recension is explicitly recognized and validated by other major Purān.as. The Bhavis.ya Purān.a (Brāhma Parva, 1.1.103–105) explicitly states: sarvān.yeva purān.āni sajñeyāni narars.abha — dvādaśaiva sahasrān.i proktānı̄ha manı̄s.ibhih. —— 103 —— punarvr.ddhim . gatānı̄ha ākhyānairvividhairvr..sa — yathā skāndam . tathā cedam . bhavis.yam . kurunandana —— 104 —— skāndam . śatasahasram . tu lokānām . jñātameva hi — bhavis.yametadrs.in.ā laks.ārddham . sam . khyayā kr.tam —— 105 —— “All the Purān.as were originally known to consist of twelve thousand verses by the wise, O best of men. Later, they expanded through various narratives—just like the Skanda

Purān.a and this Bhavis.ya Purān.a, O descendant of Kuru. The Skanda Purān.a consists of one hundred thousand (śatasahasram) verses; indeed, this is well known among the people.” Historical transmission further corroborates the antiquity and authority of the Samhitā tradition. The Kānda Purān.am, authored in the 11th century CE by the Tamil Śaiva Ācārya Kacchiyappa Śivāchāriyār, is a direct adaptation of the Śaṅkara and Sūta Samhitās of the Skanda Purān.a. This precedes the earliest citations of the Khan.d.a recension found in the works of 13th-century commentators such as Rāmānujācārya and Madhvācārya. Consequently, both the Khan.d.a and Samhitā recensions are authentic, historically transmitted branches of the Skanda Purān.a corpus, and both possess full scriptural authority (prāmān.ya).

Refutation & Contextual Analysis: The text of the Brahma Sūtras itself does not state that the Āgamas are to be rejected as a whole. Pūrva Paks.a Objection: The Brahma Sūtras declare the Pāśupata and Śaiva Āgamas to be non-Vedic (Avaidika) and completely invalid.

Refutation & Hermeneutical Context: To properly address this objection, one must distinguish between the direct text of the Brahma Sūtras and the later commentary traditions. The sūtra frequently cited in this debate is Brahma Sūtra 2.2.45: vipratis.edhācca —— 45 —— “And on account of contradictions.” When read in its proper contextual sequence (prakaran.a) alongside the preceding sūtras (Brahma Sūtras 2.2.42–44: utpattyasam . bhavāt, na ca kartuh. karan.am, and vijñānādibhāve vā tadapratis.edhah.), this discussion addresses specific metaphysical formulations (such as the origination of the Jı̄va) rather than issuing a total rejection of the Āgamic corpus itself.

This contextual interpretation aligns directly with the hermeneutical principles of Pūrva Mı̄māsā Sūtra 1.2.24: prakaran.e sambhavannapakars.o na kalpyeta, vidhyānarthakyam . hi samprati —— 24 —— “In the case of that which is compatible with its own context, detachment or removal should not be assumed; for otherwise, the injunction itself would become meaningless.” Even in commentators who vigorously debate rival metaphysical positions, the validity of Āgamic worship and practice remains intact. As Śaṅkarācārya himself notes in his commentary on the adjacent sūtras: tanna nirākriyate — “That is not refuted.” Furthermore, responding to apparent philosophical dualisms between Śruti and Āgama, the Śaiva Ācārya Jayaratha, in his commentary Tantrālokaviveka on Abhinavagupta’s Tantrāloka, expounds on the secret nature of Brahman nahi parasyām . sam. vidi tadubhayasya sthūlasūks.marūpasya kaścidvirodhah. — “In the Supreme Consciousness, there is no contradiction whatsoever between those two forms—the gross and the subtle.” The dialectical description of the Supreme Being as transcending standard logical categories is not a defect or contradiction, but a foundational feature of Brahman. Pūrva Paks.a Objection: The Āgamas are not the Vedas, and therefore they lack scriptural authority (prāmān.ya).

Establishing Śabda-Pramān.a for the Āgamas: To establish the validity of the Āgamas, we must first examine the nature of valid knowledge (jñāna) as the sole means to liberation, and then demonstrate how the Āgamas qualify as valid verbal testimony (śabda-pramān.a).

Jnāna as the Sole Means to Liberation: In the Ātmabodha (Verse 2), Ādi Śaṅkarācārya defines jñāna as the direct and indispensable cause of liberation: bodho’nyasādhanebhyo hi sāks.ānmoks.aikasādhanam — pākasya vahnitajjñāna vinā moks.o na sidhyati —— 2 —— “Compared with all other forms of discipline, Knowledge of the Self (bodha) is the one direct means for liberation. Knowledge (vidyā) alone destroys ignorance, just as light destroys deep darkness.”

Epistemological Framework of Nyāya: The Nyāya system defines valid knowledge through four foundational means of cognition (pramān.as). As stated in Nyāya Sūtra 1.1.3:

The Āptatva of Lord Śiva: To establish the Āptatva (absolute reliability and trustworthiness) of Lord Śiva as the author of the Āgamas within the Nyāya framework, three criteria must be fulfilled: complete knowledge of reality (jñāna), freedom from error or deception, and the benevolent desire to communicate truth (icchā-śakti).

1. Jñāna: Does the Deity Know Reality As It Is? In the Kailāsa Samhitā of the Śiva Purān.a (Chapter 9, Verse 21), it is established that Sadāśiva possesses direct, effortless, and omniscient perception of all existence: yadyathāvasthita vastu tattathaiva sadāśivah. — ayatnenaiva jānāti tasmātsarvajña ucyate —— “Whatever object exists, exactly in whichever way it is

situated, Sadāśiva knows it precisely in that very manner without any effort; therefore, He is called ’Omniscient’ (sarvajña).” This passage directly aligns with Ācārya Vātsyāyana’s criterion of yathādr..s.tasyārthasya (cognizing an object precisely as it exists in reality).

2. Freedom from Deception (Akhan.d.a-Yathārthatva): Pūrva Paks.a Objection: Omniscience alone is insufficient. Why assume Lord Śiva’s statements are free from error, bias, or intentional deception? To establish absolute truthfulness (akhan.d.a-yathārthatva), the source must be completely free from avidyā (ignorance) and delusion. The Śvetāśvatara Upanis.ad (4.18) defines Śiva as pure, untainted consciousness beyond all dualities and darkness: yadā tamas tanna divā na rātrir na sanna cāsac chiva eva kevalah. — tad aks.ara tat savitur varen.ya prajñā ca tasmāt prasr.tā purān.ı̄ —— 18 —— “When there is no darkness [ignorance], there is neither day nor night, neither being (sat) nor non-being (asat), but Śiva alone, absolute and pure (kevalah.). That is the Imperishable (aks.aram), that is the adorable light of Savitr., and from That issued forth ancient Wisdom (prajñā).” Because Lord Śiva is the pure, imperishable source of primal wisdom (prajñā) devoid of tamas (ignorance), his words are inherently free from error, delusion, or intent to deceive.

3. Icchā-Śakti: Śiva’s Desire to Reveal Truth: The desire to make known the truth as perceived (cikhyāpayis.ā) is demonstrated through Lord Śiva’s Icchā-Śakti (will/desire to illuminate). As revealed in the Śvetāśvatara Upanis.ad (3.18): yo brahmān.a vidadhāti pūrva yo vai vedāś ca prahin.oti tasmai — ta ha deva ātmabuddhiprakāśa mumuks.ur vai śaran.am aha prapadye —— 18 —— “To Him who at the beginning of creation brought forth Brahmā, and who verily delivered (revealed) the Vedas unto him, to that Lord, the illuminator of self-knowledge, I, desirous of liberation (mumuks.u), fly for refuge.”

The compound ātma-buddhi-prakāśam specifically characterizes Lord Śiva as the illuminator of Self-knowledge (ātmabuddhi) through revealed scriptures. If Lord Śiva is recognized as the ultimate revealer of ātma-buddhi who imparted the Vedas, there is no logical ground to deny that His secondary revelations—such as the Śaiva, Pāśupata, or Bhairava Āgamas—likewise convey ātma-buddhi. Given that illuminating spiritual knowledge is an essential attribute of Lord Śiva, and given that the Vedic corpus itself acknowledges the authority of Āgamic revelations, the Āgamas stand validated as self-authoritative (svatah.-pramān.a) through the divine Āptatva of Lord Śiva.

4. Upades.t.r.tva (Communication) & Application of Dan.d.a-pūpa Nyāya: Pūrva Paks.a Objection: Even if Lord Śiva possesses complete knowledge (jñāna) and freedom from deception, He cannot be considered an Āpta for the Āgamas if He lacks the capacity to communicate (upades..tr.tva) or transmit scripture effectively to human beings.

Refutation & Hermeneutical Resolution: This objection is directly refuted by the Śvetāśvatara Upanis.ad (3.18), which explicitly affirms Lord Śiva’s active role in transmitting supreme revelation: yo vai vedāś ca prahin.oti tasmai —— “Who indeed revealed and sent forth the Vedas unto Him (Brahmā).” Because the Upanis.ad establishes Lord Śiva as the original revealer and communicator of the entire Vedic corpus, His capacity as an active upades..tā (teacher/communicator) is indisputable. To establish the authority of the Āgamas from this premise, we apply the classical maxim of Dan.d.a-pūpa Nyāya (the Maxim of the Stick and the Cake): Dan.d.a-pūpa Nyāya dictates that if a stick and a cake are bound together, and a mouse is known to have eaten the hard stick, it is naturally and inevitably inferred that the softer cake tied to it has also been eaten. In this context, the vast, highly complex, and eternal Veda represents the dan.d.a (the greater/harder task), while the specialized Āgamas represent the pūpa (the secondary/easier task). Since it is authoritatively established that Lord Śiva revealed the monumental Vedic revelation—which is universally accepted as authoritative Śabda-Pramān.a—it follows a

fortiori (with much less effort) that His secondary, specialized revelations, such as the Śaiva and Pāśupata Āgamas, are also authoritative. If the primary and greater work (Veda) is recognized as His infallible utterance, the secondary and auxiliary work (Āgama) originating from the exact same Āpta cannot be logically rejected.

5. Identity of the Revealers (Abheda) & Extension of Āptatva to Śākta and Vais.n.ava Āgamas: The Āptatva of Devı̄ and the authority of the Śākta Āgamas need not be established independently, as the Śiva Purān.a (Vāyavı̄ya Sahitā, Uttarabhāga, Chapter 4, Verse 9) explicitly defines the complete non-difference (*abheda*) between Śiva and Devı̄: yathā śivas tathā devı̄ yathā devı̄ tathā śivah. — nānayor antara vidyāc candracandrikayor iva —— 9 —— “As is Śiva, so is the Goddess (Devı̄); as is the Goddess, so is Śiva. One should perceive no difference between them, just as there is no separation between the moon and the moonlight.” Because there is absolute identity between Śiva and Devı̄, the Āptatva established for Lord Śiva applies inherently and identically to Devı̄, thereby validating the Śākta Āgamas. Furthermore, the Rudra-hr.daya Upanis.ad (Verse 11) declares: umāśaṅkarayogo yah. sa yogo vis.n.ur ucyate — yastu tasmai namaskāra kuryād bhaktisamanvitah. —— 11 —— “The union of Umā and Śaṅkara is that very union called Vis.n.u. One should offer bowful respects to Him with devotion.” To establish the Āptatva of Lord Vis.n.u from this scriptural premise, we apply the classical maxim of Yathā-Kāran.a- Gun.a-Tathā-Kārya-Gun.a Nyāya (The Maxim that the Qualities of the Cause Manifest in the Effect): This Nyāya dictates that the qualities (gun.as) inherent in the constituent causes must necessarily manifest in the resulting union or effect. Since the constituent principles of this divine union—Śiva and Umā—are established Āptas characterized by absolute omniscience (sarvajñatva) and freedom from error, the non-

different union itself (Lord Vis.n.u) inherently possesses the complete quality of Āptatva. Consequently, the Vais.n.ava Āgamas (Pañcarātra) revealed by Lord Vis.n.u possess the exact same self-evident authority (ātma-prāmān.ya) as the Vedas and the Pāśupata Āgamas. This non-difference among manifestations extends equally to the fierce aspect of Bhairava. As stated in the Padma Purān.a (Uttara-khan.d.a, Chapter 18, Verse 128): svaya ca bhairava rūpa kr.tvā tanmadhyagah. papau — tı̄ks.n.adas..trā mahākāyās tadāsan yoginı̄gan.āh. —— 128 —— “He Himself assumed the form of Bhairava, stood among them, and drank. The groups of Yoginı̄s then became large-bodied with sharp fangs.” Therefore, in all matters concerning Āptatva and scriptural revelation, complete abheda (non-difference) must be recognized among the divine forms (Śiva, Devı̄, Vis.n.u, Bhairava) who revealed the Āgama-śāstras.

Maṅgalācaran.a & Conclusion To conclude this defense of Sarva-Āgama-Prāmān.ya, we offer our humble obeisances unto the Supreme Divine Manifestations and the Sacred Revealed Texts through the following concluding Maṅgalācaran.a: namah. śivāyābhayadāya nitya devyai ca tasye jagadı̄śvarāyai — śrı̄-bhairavāyātma-viśuddhi-dātre namo’stu sarvāgama-pūjitāya —— 1 —— “Salutations forever unto Śiva, the bestower of fearlessness; unto the Goddess (Devı̄), the Supreme Controller of the universe; unto Śrı̄ Bhairava, the purifier of the Self; and salutations unto the holy Āgamas worshipped by all.” Thus, the Āgamas stand united with the Vedas as eternal, infallible, and self-authoritative (svatah.-pramān.a) guideposts leading all seeking souls (mumuks.us) toward ultimate liberation (moks.a). —— iti śrı̄-sarvāgama-prāmān.ya-vādah. sampūrn.am ——
